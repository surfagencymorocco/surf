What Is Killer Point?

Killer Point is a long right-hand point break located about 1 km south of Anchor Point, between Taghazout and the village of Tamraght. Despite its intimidating name — derived from the killer whales (orcas) that are occasionally spotted offshore (sightings are rare and seasonal), not from the danger of the wave — Killer Point is actually a more forgiving and more accessible wave than Anchor Point on most days.

On bigger swells when Anchor Point is maxing out and dangerous, Killer Point comes into its own. The wave produces long, workable walls that can run for 300 metres on the best days, offering a ride that is less critical and more cruise-oriented than its famous neighbour to the north. For intermediate surfers looking for a proper point-break experience without the intensity and reef hazards of Anchor Point, Killer Point is the sweet spot of the Taghazout coast.

The name has become part of local lore. Fishermen from Tamraght have reported orca sightings in these waters for generations, particularly in the winter months when the animals follow tuna migrations along the Atlantic coast. While you are unlikely to see a killer whale from the lineup, the name serves as a reminder that this stretch of Moroccan coastline is wild, alive, and deeply connected to the ocean.

Did You Know?

Killer Point was relatively unknown outside Morocco until the late 1990s when surf explorers began venturing south from Anchor Point. Today it is widely considered one of the best intermediate point breaks in North Africa — a wave that offers long, cruisy rides with a more manageable take-off than Anchor Point.

Wave Characteristics & How It Breaks

Killer Point breaks as a right-hand point over a rock and sand bottom. The wave peels along a headland that juts out from the arid Moroccan coastline, creating a natural amphitheatre for long, open-faced walls. Understanding the three distinct sections of the wave is key to getting the most out of your session.

The Take-Off Zone

The wave starts on the outside, near a prominent rock shelf that marks the take-off zone. The wave jacks up quickly here — faster than many first-timers expect — before transitioning into the mid-section. On bigger days (5 ft+), the take-off requires commitment: paddle hard, pop up decisively, and set a high line immediately. The rock shelf means the water is shallower here than it looks, so avoid nose-diving.

The Mid-Section

Once past the initial drop, the wave opens up into a long, sloping wall that is the true heart of Killer Point. This section offers 100–150 metres of open face perfect for carving, cutbacks, and drawn-out bottom turns. The wall is rarely hollow — instead it presents a clean, groomed canvas that gives you time to read the wave and set up your manoeuvres. This is where intermediate surfers can build confidence on a point-break and advanced surfers can practice flow and style.

The Inside Reform

As the wave approaches the inside, it often backs off before reforming into a shorter, softer section near the shore. This reform is playful rather than powerful — ideal for floaters, roundhouse cutbacks, or simply cruising to the beach. It is also the easiest place to kick out and start the paddle back to the peak.

Best Swell, Tide & Wind for Killer Point

Killer Point is a swell magnet but a picky one. When the conditions align, it is arguably one of the best waves in the Taghazout area for long, satisfying rides. Here is exactly what to look for.

ConditionOptimalAcceptablePoor
Swell DirectionWNW (280°–310°)NW (310°–330°)S or N
Swell Size4–8 ft @ 12–16s3–4 ft or 8–10 ft<2 ft or >12 ft
WindLight offshore (E/SE)Glass / light cross-shoreStrong onshore (NW/W)
TideMid-tide risingMid-tide fallingExtreme low or dead high

Swell direction is the single most important variable for Killer Point. A west-northwest swell in the 280–310° range wraps perfectly into the bay and lights up the point. If the swell is too north (320°+), it passes by the headland; too south, and it closes out across the bay. Killer Point is slightly more tolerant of westerly swell angles than Anchor Point, which is one reason it often works on days when Anchor is too big or too crossed up.

Wind makes or breaks the session. The ideal wind is a light offshore from the east or southeast — common on winter mornings before the sea breeze kicks in. This grooms the face and holds the wave open, creating the glassy, oil-smooth walls that Killer Point is famous for. When the wind swings onshore (northwest), the face gets bumpy and the magic evaporates fast.

Tide is critical. At dead low tide, the rocks near the take-off become hazardously exposed and the wave can become sectiony and unpredictable. At dead high, the swell can swamp out and lose its shape across the reef. The golden window is a rising mid-tide, which pushes water over the rocks and gives the wave its ideal combination of push, shape, and forgiveness.

What Skill Level Do You Need?

Killer Point is more accessible than Anchor Point, but it is still a proper point break that demands respect. Here is an honest assessment by skill level:

  • Beginner (never surfed or <1 year): Do not paddle out at Killer Point. The rocky entry, strong currents, and size of the wave on most days make it unsuitable for new surfers. Visit Imsouane Bay or the Tamraght beach breaks instead.
  • Intermediate (1–3 years, comfortable on green waves): Killer Point is an excellent progression wave for intermediates. On small to medium days (2–4 ft), the mid-section and inside reform offer long, forgiving walls with plenty of time to practice turns. Stay away on bigger swells (6 ft+) until you are confident in overhead conditions. Booking an Intermediate Surf Camp with a guide who knows the wave is the smartest way to experience it.
  • Advanced (3+ years, comfortable in overhead waves): On a solid 5–8 ft day, Killer Point offers one of the longest, most satisfying rides in Morocco. You can link the outside take-off through the mid-section and into the inside reform for a 300-metre wave — the kind of ride that justifies the entire trip. Best Seasons to Surf Killer Point

The Moroccan surf season follows the North Atlantic swell cycle. Here is what to expect month by month at Killer Point:

  • October–November: The season kicks off with consistent head-high swells, warm weather (20–25°C), and manageable crowds. An excellent window for intermediate surfers to get their first taste of a Moroccan point break. Killer Point is especially fun in this window — not too big, not too crowded, with clean conditions on most days.
  • December–February: Peak season. The biggest, most powerful swells of the year arrive, and this is when Killer Point truly shines. While Anchor Point becomes a heavy, intimidating wall of water, Killer Point scales up gracefully — producing long, clean walls in the 6–8 ft range. Water drops to 16–17°C, so bring your 4/3 mm wetsuit and your best paddling fitness.
  • March–April: Still very consistent with slightly smaller swells on average. Water warms (17–19°C), the weather is stunning, and the crowds thin out. Many local guides consider March the best month overall for Killer Point — glassy mornings, reliable swell, and empty lineups.
  • May–September: Summer season. Swells are smaller and less frequent, but head-high days still happen. Warm water (20–23°C) means a 3/2 mm wetsuit or even a spring suit. Killer Point is very quiet in summer — you might share the lineup with just a handful of locals. A great window for relaxed, uncrowded sessions.

How to Get to Killer Point

Killer Point is located about 1 km south of Anchor Point, between Taghazout and Tamraght village. The spot is roughly 45 minutes north of Agadir Al-Massira Airport (AGA), which has direct connections from Paris, London, Warsaw, Berlin, Amsterdam, Barcelona, Brussels, and other European cities (as of 2026 — routes change seasonally).

From Taghazout centre, head south along the coastal road (N1) towards Tamraght. Look for the dirt parking area on the cliff above the point — it is less formal than Anchor Point's car park, but you will usually see a handful of surf vans and rental cars pulled up. From the parking area, a well-worn path leads down the cliff to the take-off zone. The walk takes about 5 minutes and offers a perfect vantage point to check the conditions before you commit to the paddle.

If you are staying at a surf camp in Taghazout or Tamraght, your guides will handle transport. This is the biggest advantage of booking a camp: local guides check conditions at dawn and know exactly when Killer Point is the right call. Book your surf trip and let us handle the logistics.

The Lineup — Local Etiquette & Crowd

Killer Point has a noticeably more relaxed vibe than Anchor Point. Fewer surfers, more space, and a wave that offers enough length for everyone to get their share. That said, the lineup still operates on a hierarchy that visiting surfers should respect:

  • Respect the locals. Many Tamraght residents have been surfing Killer Point since childhood. Watch how they position themselves and follow their lead. A smile and a greeting in French or Arabic go a long way.
  • Sit on the shoulder, not the peak. If it is your first time at Killer Point, start on the shoulder. Let the regulars sit deeper. There will be plenty of waves.
  • Do not paddle around the pack. The peak at Killer Point is well-defined — usually near the rock shelf on the outside. Paddling wide and cutting inside the rotation is the quickest way to burn goodwill.
  • Share waves on the inside. Because the wave is so long, it is common to share a wave with someone who has paddled back out. If you see a surfer on the inside who can pick up the reform, let them know.
Important — Respect the Locals

Moroccan surf culture is warm but demands respect. Killer Point is less competitive than Anchor Point, which makes a good attitude even more appreciated. Greet people, wait your turn, and never assume priority. Booking a camp with local guides who know the regulars personally is the best way to integrate smoothly into the lineup.

Hazards & What to Watch Out For

  • Rocks at low tide. The take-off zone sits over a rock shelf that becomes dangerously shallow at dead low tide. If you wipe out on the take-off, cover your head and try to fall flat rather than diving head-first. Booties are strongly recommended.
  • Strong rip on big swells. When the swell hits 6 ft+, a persistent rip runs along the point, sweeping surfers south towards Tamraght. Do not fight the rip head-on — paddle wide and use the inside channel to get back to the peak.
  • Sea urchins near the entry point. The rocky entry path harbours sea urchins, particularly at low tide when they are exposed or near the surface. Reef booties are your best friend here — even a 2 mm pair can save you from a painful (and trip-ending) encounter.
  • Hold-downs on overhead days. At 6 ft+, Killer Point can pack enough power for two-wave hold-downs. Stay calm, conserve your energy, and wait for the turbulence to subside before surfacing. If you are not comfortable in overhead surf, stick to smaller days.
Common Mistake

The most common mistake at Killer Point? Paddling out without watching the wave first. The peak can shift up to 30 metres depending on the tide and swell angle. Spend 10 minutes on the cliff path studying the sets before you commit to the paddle — it is the best 10 minutes you will spend all session.

Local Knowledge

When the swell is big and clean, the take-off at Killer Point shifts slightly further north — closer to the rock shelf. When it is smaller, the peak migrates south into slightly deeper water. Watch where the local regulars are sitting — they have been reading this wave for decades.

Killer Point vs Anchor Point — Key Differences

Taghazout is blessed with two world-class point breaks less than 2 km apart. Understanding the differences between Killer Point and Anchor Point will help you decide where to surf on any given day — or better yet, which one suits your skill level and style.

FeatureKiller PointAnchor Point
Wave LengthUp to 300 m (typically 150–250 m)200–500 m (typically 250–400 m)
DifficultyIntermediate–AdvancedAdvanced
CrowdModerateBusy (winter)
BottomRock + sandReef + rock
Best TideMidMid-tide rising
VibeRelaxedCompetitive
Best ForLong cruisy ridesSteep, powerful walls & barrels

The simplest way to think about it: Anchor Point is the performance wave — steep, fast, and demanding. Killer Point is the cruiser — long, open, and rewarding. On a big winter swell when Anchor is a double-overhead washing machine, Killer Point is often the best wave in the region. On smaller, cleaner days, both waves light up and it comes down to personal preference. The best strategy? Stay at a surf camp with guides who check both spots at dawn and make the call based on the conditions — not the reputation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What level of surfer is Killer Point for?
Killer Point is best for intermediate to advanced surfers. On small, clean days (2–3 ft), confident intermediates can enjoy the mid-section and inside reform. On bigger swells (5 ft+), the take-off becomes steeper and it is better suited to advanced surfers.
When is the best time to surf Killer Point?
October through April, with December to February producing the biggest and most consistent swells. Dawn patrol on a rising mid-tide with a WNW swell and light offshore wind is the ideal combination.
Is Killer Point dangerous?
Killer Point is generally less dangerous than Anchor Point. The name comes from killer whales occasionally spotted offshore, not from the wave itself. However, rocks at low tide, strong currents on big swells, and sea urchins near the entry point are real hazards to be aware of.
How long is the ride at Killer Point?
Up to 300 metres on a good day. On the best swells, linking the outside take-off through the mid-section and into the inside reform can produce one of the longest point-break rides in Morocco.
Can beginners surf Killer Point?
No. While Killer Point is more forgiving than Anchor Point, the rocky entry, strong current, and size of the wave on most days make it unsuitable for beginners. Head to Imsouane Bay or the Tamraght beach breaks instead.
What is the difference between Killer Point and Anchor Point?
Killer Point is longer (up to 300 m), more mellow, and has a more relaxed crowd than Anchor Point. It breaks over rock and sand (versus Anchor's reef), works best on mid-tide, and thrives on bigger swells when Anchor Point maxes out. Anchor Point is steeper, faster, and better suited to advanced surfers hunting barrels.
How far is Killer Point from Agadir airport?
About 45 minutes by car. Killer Point is located roughly 1 km south of Anchor Point, between Taghazout and Tamraght village. Most surf camps include airport transfers.
Is there parking at Killer Point?
Yes — there is a dirt parking area on the cliff above the point. It is smaller and more informal than Anchor Point's car park. Walk down the path from the cliff to reach the take-off zone.
What board should I bring to Killer Point?
A 6'0"–6'6" shortboard for most days, or a 7'+ midlength for smaller, cruisier sessions. The wave's open face rewards boards with a bit of volume for smooth carving.
Are there facilities at Killer Point?
No — Killer Point itself has no facilities. The nearest cafés, restaurants, and shops are a 5–10 minute walk away in Tamraght village or a short drive back to Taghazout centre.
Can I surf Killer Point at dawn?
Yes — dawn patrol at Killer Point is highly recommended. Early mornings bring glassy conditions with light offshore winds, fewer crowds, and stunning sunrises over the Atlantic.
Is Killer Point better than Anchor Point?
It depends on the day. Killer Point is often the superior call when the swell is big (6–8 ft+) and Anchor Point becomes too heavy or maxed out. On smaller, cleaner days, Anchor Point offers steeper, more critical waves. Each has its strengths — the best strategy is to have a local guide who checks both spots each morning.
