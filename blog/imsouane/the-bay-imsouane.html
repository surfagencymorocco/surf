What Is The Bay?

The Bay at Imsouane is not just a wave — it is a phenomenon. This right-hand point break peels along the southern edge of Imsouane's wide sandy bay, producing the longest rideable wave in Africa and one of the longest in the world. On a clean winter day, a single wave can carry you for up to 800 metres — well over a minute of continuous surfing from take-off to kick-out.

But The Bay's magic is not just about length. It is about accessibility. Unlike most point breaks, which break over reef or rock and demand a certain skill level, The Bay breaks over sand. The take-off is gentle. The wall peels slowly. The paddle-out is easy. All of this adds up to a wave that absolute beginners, longboarders, and surfers of every ability can enjoy — often side by side in the same session.

The Bay sits at the southern end of Imsouane village, just past the small river mouth. The take-off is at the point near the base of the cliff, and the wave peels northwards into the bay, finishing near the village beach. From the cliff above, you can watch the entire 800-metre ride unfold — a mesmerising spectacle that draws photographers and spectators alike.

Did You Know?

The Bay's legendary length comes from its unique geography. The swell wraps around the headland at exactly the right angle to create a peeling wall that reforms continuously as it travels along the sandbank. Most surf breaks only offer one or two sections — The Bay effectively offers five or six connected sections on a good day.

How The Bay Breaks — Three Sections

Section 1: The Outside Take-Off

The wave begins its journey at the far southern point, where the headland meets the bay. On a clean swell, the outside take-off is the steepest and most critical part of the ride. The wave jacks up to waist-to-head-high and peels right, offering an open face that longboarders use for cross-steps and noserides. This is where you want to be if you are hunting maximum ride length — catch the wave here, and you have the entire 800-metre canvas ahead of you.

Section 2: The Mid-Section (The Sweet Spot)

After the initial drop, the wave transitions into its longest, most consistent section — a rolling, waist-high wall that peels for 300 to 500 metres. This is The Bay's sweet spot: the wave is fast enough to be fun but slow enough for beginners to feel completely in control. Intermediates can practise turns and cutbacks, while longboarders can settle into extended noserides. The mid-section is where most of the magic happens.

Section 3: The Inside Reform

As the wave approaches the beach and the river mouth, it often backs off before reforming into a shorter, softer wave closer to shore. This inside reform is where most surf schools run their beginner lessons — the white water is gentle, the water is shallow enough to stand up, and the rides are long enough to feel the thrill without the fear. Even experienced surfers enjoy milking the inside section all the way to the sand for a satisfying full-stop finish.

Tide plays a bigger role at The Bay than most people realise. At dead low tide, the wave becomes steeper and more hollow at the take-off — fun for experienced surfers but less forgiving for beginners. At dead high tide, the wave can become fat and slow, losing some of its peel. The sweet spot is a rising mid-tide, especially in the morning, when the incoming water pushes over the sandbank and creates the smoothest, longest face possible.

Guides' Formula for the Perfect Bay Day

NW groundswell at 3–4 ft with a 12–14 second period + light offshore wind from the east + rising mid-tide starting at 7 AM = 800-metre rides all morning. If you see all three factors aligning in the forecast, cancel your plans and paddle out.

Why The Bay Is a Beginner's Paradise

If you were to design the perfect beginner wave in a laboratory, you would end up with something that looks a lot like The Bay. Here is why it works so well for newcomers:

  • Sand bottom. No reef, no rocks, no urchins. Falling off is safe and painless. This removes the single biggest fear most beginners have — hurting themselves on the bottom.
  • Slow, predictable peel. The wave breaks at a gentle pace. You have time to get to your feet, find your balance, and look down the line. Nothing feels rushed.
  • Long white-water rides. Even if you stay in the broken white water, the reform continues for hundreds of metres. You can practise standing up and balancing on a wave that keeps going and going, building confidence with every metre.
  • Easy paddle-out. A gentle channel runs along the inside, making the paddle back to the take-off relatively effortless. This is critical for beginners, who often spend more energy paddling out than riding waves at other breaks.
  • Progressive difficulty. The inside reform is gentle and forgiving. As you improve, move further up the point — the take-off gets progressively steeper, allowing you to challenge yourself at your own pace.
  • Warm water (most of the year). From May to November, water temperatures are 19–23°C, meaning you can spend hours in the water without getting cold — crucial for long beginner sessions.
Coaching Tip

At The Bay, we always start beginners at the inside section near the river mouth. The water is waist-deep, the waves are broken (white water), and the instructor is right there. By day two or three, we move students to the mid-section to catch green (unbroken) waves. This progression is one of the fastest in the world — most students ride a green wave by day three.

Why Longboarders Love The Bay

The Bay is routinely listed in the top 10 longboard waves on the planet, and for good reason. Its long, open face gives longboarders the time and space to do what longboarding is really about: style. Noserides that last 10 to 15 seconds. Cross-steps with room to spare. Drop-knee cutbacks on a waist-high wall that just keeps giving.

The wave's speed is perfectly matched to a traditional 9'–10' log. Unlike a beach break, where the wave jacks up quickly and closes out fast, The Bay unfolds slowly. This lets you position yourself early, pick your line, and ride with intention rather than reacting. It is the difference between playing a song at double-speed and playing it at the right tempo.

For longboarders looking for variety, the inside reform near the river mouth often produces a shorter, punchier section that is perfect for practising turns and finishing moves. You can noseride for 300 metres on the mid-section, then throw a big roundhouse cutback at the inside bank — the best of both worlds in a single wave.

Local Knowledge

The sandbank at The Bay shifts seasonally. In winter, the bank sits further south near the point, producing the classic long rides. In summer, the bank migrates north towards the river mouth, making the wave shorter but punchier. If you are visiting in summer, do not expect 800-metre rides — but do expect a fun, crowd-free session with a different character.

How to Catch the Longest Ride

Scoring The Bay's legendary 800-metre ride is not just about luck — it is about positioning, timing, and technique. Here is how to give yourself the best chance:

  1. Position yourself at the far outside peak. Walk all the way to the southern end of the point, near the cliff, and paddle to the deepest take-off spot. This is where the journey begins.
  2. Sit deeper than you think you need to. The wave looks mellower from the shoulder, but sitting deeper (closer to the peak) means you catch it earlier and get the full canvas ahead of you.
  3. Take off early and hold a high line. Do not drop straight down and bottom-turn — the wave will lose its shape. Instead, angle your take-off and stay high on the face. The wave will carry you.
  4. Do not pump (unless you have to). The Bay generates its own speed from the peeling wall. Excessive pumping wastes energy and can cause you to outrun the pocket. Trust the wave.
  5. Look far down the line. Keep your eyes on the section 30 to 50 metres ahead of you. This helps you read the wave and adjust your line before you reach each section.
  6. Pick the right tide. A rising mid-tide is the sweet spot. The incoming water smooths the face and connects the sections. Dead high or dead low tide makes it harder to link the full ride.

The Bay and Anchor Point are like two siblings with completely different personalities. The Bay is the gentle older sibling who wants you to have a good time no matter your skill level. Anchor Point is the intense younger sibling who demands your best and rewards you with something unforgettable — but only if you are ready. Both are world-class waves. Which one you choose depends on what kind of experience you are after.

Hazards & What to Watch For

The Bay is remarkably safe compared to most surf breaks, but no wave is entirely without risk:

  • Rip current (big swells). On days over 5 ft, a rip can develop near the harbour wall at the north end of the bay. If caught, paddle parallel to shore (south along the beach), not against the rip.
  • Other surfers. The Bay's long rides mean surfers are spread out, but collisions can happen if someone drops in on the outside while you are riding the inside. Always check left and right before you commit to a wave.
  • Fishing boats. Small wooden boats occasionally motor through the lineup. They have right of way — paddle clear and give them space.
  • Sunburn. This sounds trivial, but the Moroccan sun, combined with water reflection and long sessions (because the rides are so long), leads to severe sunburn faster than you expect. High-SPF zinc is non-negotiable.
Common Mistake

The most common mistake at The Bay? Beginners paddling straight to the outside take-off on their first day because they "want the long ride." The outside take-off on a head-high day is not for first-timers. Start at the inside, work your way down the point gradually, and earn the long ride when you are ready.

Our Team's Top 5 Tips for The Bay
  • Arrive at sunrise. Not just for the empty lineup — the light is spectacular, and the offshore wind is at its cleanest.
  • Bring a longboard, even if you usually ride a shortboard. You will get more waves, longer rides, and a bigger smile.
  • Watch the wave from the cliff for 10 minutes before paddling out. Note where the sets are breaking and which section is working best.
  • Respect the longboarders. They can catch the wave 50 metres before you — do not paddle inside them.
  • After your session, walk to the harbour for grilled sardines. It is the best post-surf meal in Morocco and costs about 25 MAD (â‚¬2.50).

A Perfect Bay Session — Timeline

06:15

Wake up. Grab a coffee from the café above The Bay and watch the sunrise from the cliff. Check the conditions — the entire wave is visible from the viewpoint.

06:45

Dawn patrol. Glassy conditions, empty lineup. Paddle to the outside peak for the longest rides of the day.

08:30

The crowd starts arriving. Shift to the mid-section — still plenty of waves, just slightly less distance.

10:00

Breakfast. Msemen pancakes, fresh orange juice, and a second coffee at a café terrace overlooking the point. Watch the sets while you eat.

11:00

Second session (optional). The tide is high now, the wave is a bit slower. Perfect for practising noserides or just cruising.

13:00

Lunch at the harbour. Grilled fish, Moroccan salad, bread, olives. Walk through the village afterwards.

Sunset

Evening session or sunset-watch from the cliff. The golden light on The Bay is unforgettable. Dinner at a local restaurant.

The Bay Session Checklist

  • Check the surf forecast (Windy, Windguru, or Surfline) — look for NW swell, 2–5 ft, light E/SE wind
  • Check the tide chart — aim for a rising mid-tide in the morning
  • Bring a foamie (if a beginner), longboard (if experienced), or funboard (for versatility)
  • Appropriate wetsuit — 4/3mm winter, 3/2mm shoulder, boardshorts summer
  • Booties not necessary (sand bottom), but bring them if you plan to walk on the rocks near the point
  • High-SPF zinc sunscreen — reapply after your session
  • Water bottle — sessions at The Bay can easily last 2–3 hours
  • Cash (MAD) — for post-surf grilled fish and mint tea at the harbour
  • Watch the wave for 10 minutes from the cliff before paddling out — note the peak, the sections, and the crowd
  • Surf with a buddy or a guide, especially if you are new to the spot

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is the longest ride at The Bay Imsouane?
On an ideal day — clean NW swell, rising mid-tide, light offshore wind — rides of 600 to 800 metres are possible. The record is reportedly over 1,000 metres on an exceptional winter swell, though rides of 300 to 500 metres are more typical on an average day.
Can complete beginners surf The Bay?
Yes — The Bay is widely considered one of the best beginner waves in the world. The sand bottom, slow peel, and easy paddle-out make it ideal for first-timers. Most beginners stand up within their first hour and ride green waves by day two or three.
What is the best tide for The Bay?
The Bay works on most tides, but a rising mid-tide (incoming) produces the longest, smoothest rides. Dead low tide can make the take-off slightly steeper and more hollow, which is fun for experienced surfers but harder for beginners. Dead high tide can sometimes make the wave fat and slow.
What board should I ride at The Bay?
Beginners should use an 8'+ foam board — the extra volume makes catching waves and popping up much easier. Longboarders should bring a 9'+ log for classic noseriding. Intermediates can enjoy a 7'–8' funboard or mid-length. Shortboards work on bigger days but are not the ideal tool for The Bay's slow pace.
How does The Bay compare to Anchor Point?
The Bay is longer (300–800m vs. 200–500m), slower, more forgiving, and has a sand bottom — perfect for beginners and longboarders. Anchor Point is steeper, faster, breaks over reef, and is for intermediate to advanced surfers. The Bay gives you a minute-long cruise; Anchor Point gives you a high-performance drive.
Is The Bay crowded?
In peak winter (December–February), The Bay can have 20–40 surfers spread across the lineup. However, because the wave is so long, the crowd naturally distributes along the point. It rarely feels as packed as Anchor Point on a good day. Summer (May–September) is much quieter, often with fewer than 10 people in the water.
Does The Bay work at all tides?
Generally yes — The Bay is one of the most tide-tolerant waves in Morocco. It is rideable at low, mid, and high tide, though the character of the wave changes. Low tide is steeper and more hollow (fun for experienced riders), mid-rising is the smoothest and longest, and high tide can be slower but still very rideable.
Can I surf The Bay on a shortboard?
Yes, but it is not the best board for this wave. On a bigger swell (4–5 ft), a shortboard can work well, especially on the outside take-off. However, The Bay's slow pace means you will spend more energy paddling and less time doing the turns a shortboard is designed for. A fish or hybrid is a better compromise.
Is there a river mouth at The Bay?
Yes — at the southern end of The Bay, a small river (oued) meets the ocean. The sandbank here shifts with the seasons, and the wave often reforms into a fun, punchy inside section near the river mouth. This inside reform is a favourite spot for beginners taking lessons.
What wildlife might I see at The Bay?
Dolphins are occasionally spotted in the bay, and the cliffs are home to seabirds including gulls and cormorants. The fishing harbour attracts cats and the occasional dog. There are no dangerous sharks in this area — the most common marine life you will encounter are small fish and the fishermen hauling in their morning catch.
Can I take surf photos at The Bay?
Yes — the cliff above the southern side of The Bay provides an excellent elevated viewpoint for photography. A telephoto lens (200mm+) is ideal for capturing long rides from the cliff. Alternatively, the beach at the inside section lets you shoot from water level. The best light is early morning and late afternoon.
Is The Bay safe for swimming when not surfing?
Generally yes, especially at the inside section near the beach. However, on bigger swells (5 ft+), the current along the point can be strong. Swim between the flags if lifeguards are present (summer months), and always be aware of rip currents near the harbour wall on the north side of the bay.
