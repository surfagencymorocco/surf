Who Are the Berber (Amazigh) People?

When you arrive in Taghazout, Tamraght, or Imsouane, you are stepping into a world shaped not primarily by Arab culture — as many visitors expect — but by the much older civilisation of the Amazigh, or Berber people. The Imazighen (plural of Amazigh), whose name means "free people" or "noble people," are the indigenous inhabitants of North Africa, with a continuous presence in Morocco stretching back at least 4,000 years.

The Berbers predate the arrival of Phoenicians, Romans, Arabs, and French colonists. Their culture has survived wave after wave of outside influence, adapting and absorbing elements while maintaining a distinct identity. Today, between 40 and 60 per cent of Morocco's population is of Berber origin, and Berber culture is particularly strong and visible in the Souss-Massa region, which encompasses Agadir, Taghazout, Tamraght, Paradise Valley, and the Anti-Atlas Mountains.

Did You Know?

The word "Berber" comes from the Greek "barbaros" (barbarian) — a term imposed by outsiders to describe people who did not speak Greek. The people themselves have always called themselves "Amazigh" (singular) or "Imazighen" (plural). While "Berber" is still widely used internationally, using "Amazigh" is more respectful and increasingly preferred.

A Brief History — 4,000 Years on This Coast

The Atlantic coast of what is now Morocco has been inhabited by Berber communities for millennia. The Phoenicians established trading posts here around 1000 BCE, followed by the Romans, who called the region Mauretania Tingitana. Through it all, the Berber people maintained their identity, language, and customs. The arrival of Islam in the 7th century brought Arabic language and Islamic faith, both of which were gradually adopted by Berber communities, but Amazigh identity never disappeared.

The Souss region — the area around Agadir, Taghazout, and the Anti-Atlas — has been a Berber heartland throughout history. The villages you see perched on hillsides, the terraced agriculture climbing the mountain slopes, and the fishing boats pulled up on beaches all reflect traditions that have been practised on this coast for thousands of years. The surf camps that now operate in these villages are, in many cases, run by families whose ancestors have lived on this same stretch of coast for countless generations.

Tamazight — The Language of the Free People

The Berber language, Tamazight, has been an official language of Morocco since 2011, alongside Arabic. In the Souss region around Taghazout, the local variant is called Tachelhit (often referred to as "Souss" or "Chleuh"). It is spoken by millions of people across southern Morocco and is the language you will hear in the streets, markets, and cafés of Taghazout and Tamraght.

Tamazight is written in the Tifinagh script — one of the oldest alphabets still in use anywhere in the world, with origins dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE. You will see Tifinagh letters on road signs, shop fronts, and official buildings throughout the Souss region. The distinctive geometric characters are a powerful visual reminder that you are in Amazigh country.

"In our villages, when you greet an elder with 'Salam alaykum' and then say 'Manzaknin?' — 'How are you?' in Tachelhit — their face lights up. It shows respect. It shows you see them, not just the waves."

— Hassan, SurfAgencyMorocco guide, Taghazout

Berber Hospitality — The Heart of the Culture

If you take one thing away from this article, let it be this: Berber hospitality is legendary for a reason. In Amazigh culture, a guest is considered a blessing, not a burden. The tradition of generous hospitality stems from the harsh realities of mountain and desert life, where a traveller denied shelter could face genuine danger. This ethos has survived into the modern era and defines the experience of visiting Morocco's surf villages.

If you are invited into a Berber home — and if you spend any time in the small villages around Taghazout, you may well be — you will almost certainly be offered mint tea at a minimum, and very likely a full meal. Accepting is important: refusing food or drink can be interpreted as rejecting the host's hospitality. Even if you are not hungry, take a glass of tea, take a small portion of food, and express your appreciation warmly. The gesture matters more than how much you consume.

This hospitality extends to the surf community. The Berber guides who run surf camps in Taghazout, Tamraght, and Imsouane bring this cultural value to their work — their genuine warmth and welcome are not a commercial act but an expression of who they are as Amazigh people.

The Mint Tea Ceremony

Moroccan mint tea — known locally as "atay" — is far more than a beverage. It is a ritual, a social bond, and the universal symbol of welcome. The preparation and serving of tea follow a precise tradition: green tea leaves (usually Chinese gunpowder green tea) are steeped with generous handfuls of fresh mint and a substantial amount of sugar, then poured from a height into small glasses to create a thin layer of foam on top.

The tea is typically served three times, and there is a proverb about each pour: "The first glass is as bitter as life, the second as strong as love, and the third as gentle as death." You will be offered tea everywhere — at your surf camp, in shops, during casual conversations with locals. Accepting and drinking at least one glass is an essential gesture of social connection. Even if you take your tea without sugar at home, embrace the sweetness here — refusing sugar is a losing battle in Morocco.

The Tea Ritual in Practice
  • Tea is always served to guests first, starting with the eldest or most honoured person.
  • Hold the small glass by the rim (the top) — the bottom gets hot.
  • Drink at least one full glass. It is acceptable to place your hand over the glass and say "shukran" (thank you) to politely decline a refill.
  • After a surf session, mint tea at a local café is a sacred ritual — do not skip it.

The Weekly Souk — More Than a Market

The souk (traditional market) is the social and economic heart of Berber community life. In rural Morocco, each town and large village hosts a weekly souk on a designated day, and people travel from surrounding villages to buy, sell, and socialise. The souk is where you go for fresh produce, spices, livestock, clothing, household goods, and — perhaps most importantly — to catch up with friends and relatives.

For surf travellers, the most accessible and vibrant souk is the Tuesday souk in Aourir, a village just south of Taghazout known as "Banana Village" for the banana plantations that surround it. This is not a tourist-oriented market — it is a real, working souk where locals do their weekly shopping. You will find mountains of fresh fruit and vegetables, spices piled in colourful pyramids, live chickens, handmade Berber carpets, traditional clothing, and much more. The energy is chaotic, colourful, and unforgettable.

If you visit a souk, here are a few things to know: haggling is expected but should be done with a smile and good humour. It is a social interaction, not a confrontation. Start at about half the asking price and meet somewhere in the middle. Dress modestly — the souk is a traditional space, and beachwear is inappropriate. And always ask before taking photographs — many people, especially older women, do not want to be photographed.

How Berber Culture Shapes the Surf Villages

Taghazout, Tamraght, Imsouane, and the smaller villages along this coast were Berber fishing and farming communities long before the first surfers arrived in the 1960s. The surf culture that has developed here is layered on top of — and heavily influenced by — Berber traditions. The surf camps are often family-run operations where the owners' grandparents fished the same waters that now draw wave riders from around the world.

This cultural foundation shapes the surf experience in tangible ways. The communal meals at surf camps — long tables piled with tagines, fresh salads, and bread — are an extension of the Berber tradition of shared eating. The relationship between guides and guests is built on the Amazigh value of hospitality rather than a purely commercial transaction. The rhythm of the day — early rise for dawn patrol, a long lunch and rest during the heat of the afternoon, evening gatherings on rooftops — mirrors the agricultural and fishing rhythms that have governed life on this coast for centuries.

Local Knowledge

Many of the place names you will hear as a surfer are Amazigh words. "Taghazout" likely derives from the Berber word for "gorge" or "ravine." "Tamraght" may come from a Berber word meaning "salt." "Imsouane" means "mouth" in Tachelhit — a reference to the bay's position at the mouth of a valley. Learning a few of these etymologies connects you more deeply to the place you are surfing.

Symbols & Traditions to Recognise

  • The Amazigh Flag: Three horizontal stripes — blue (the Atlantic and Mediterranean), green (the land and mountains), and yellow (the Sahara). In the centre is the red Tifinagh letter "yaz" (&#xe041;), which looks like a human figure and represents the free man. You will see this flag on buildings, cars, and clothing throughout the Souss region.
  • The Tifinagh Alphabet: The ancient Berber script appears on road signs, shop names, and official buildings. The writing system dates back over 2,000 years and was traditionally carved into stone. Today, it is taught in Moroccan schools alongside Arabic and French.
  • Berber Carpets and Textiles: Handwoven Berber rugs and blankets are among the most prized crafts in Morocco. Each design tells a story — the patterns, known as "tizra," encode family histories, protective symbols, and natural elements. The women who weave them often sing while they work. The weekly souk is a good place to see authentic pieces, though serious buyers should visit a reputable cooperative.
  • Argan Oil: The argan tree grows almost exclusively in southwestern Morocco, in the very region around Taghazout and Tamraght. Berber women have produced argan oil — for cooking and cosmetics — for centuries using traditional hand-cracking and stone-grinding methods. You will see argan cooperatives along the roads between Agadir and Essaouira.

What Visitors Should Know & Respect

  • Dress modestly away from the beach: In Taghazout and Tamraght, beachwear is acceptable on the sand and in surf-oriented cafés. Once you leave the immediate beach area — especially in smaller villages, the souk, or when visiting someone's home — cover shoulders and knees. Women do not need to cover their hair (that is a personal religious choice, not a requirement for visitors), but a scarf or sarong is useful for covering up when needed.
  • Greet people properly: "Salam alaykum" (peace be upon you) is the universal greeting. The response is "Wa alaykum salam." A handshake is standard between men; between men and women, wait for the woman to extend her hand first — some women prefer not to shake hands with men for religious reasons, and a hand over the heart with a nod and smile is a perfectly respectful alternative.
  • Ask before photographing: Always. Especially of people, and especially in the souk or in villages. A smile and a gesture toward your camera is usually enough to check. If someone declines, respect it without question.
  • During Ramadan: Avoid eating, drinking, or smoking in public during daylight hours out of respect for those fasting. Surf camps continue to operate and will provide meals for guests in private — just be discreet.
  • Friday is the holy day: The midday Friday prayer (around 12:30–1:30 PM) is the most important of the week. Many shops and businesses close during this time. Plan your errands accordingly.

A Day in a Berber Village

05:00

The first call to prayer (Fajr) echoes from the mosque minarets. In farming villages, families rise early to tend animals before the heat of the day.

07:00–09:00

Morning bread baking. Women prepare fresh bread in traditional clay ovens. The smell of baking bread mingles with the sea air in coastal villages. Breakfast is simple: bread, olive oil, honey, and mint tea.

10:00–13:00

Market or field work. Men and women work in the fields, tend argan trees, or head to the souk. In fishing villages, the morning's catch is brought in and sold directly on the beach.

13:00

Midday meal — the largest of the day. Couscous on Fridays (the traditional holy-day meal), tagine on other days. The family eats together from shared dishes, using bread to scoop food.

14:00–17:00

Rest during the heat. Shops close, streets empty. This is the time for naps, tea with neighbours, and quiet. Surfers hit the afternoon session or relax on rooftop terraces.

18:00–Sunset

Evening social life. Cafés fill with men drinking tea and playing cards. Women visit neighbours. The village comes alive as the heat fades. This is the best time to wander and absorb the rhythm of daily life.

Cultural Respect Checklist

  • Learn basic greetings: "Salam alaykum" (hello), "Shukran" (thank you), "La bas?" (how are you?)
  • Carry a scarf or sarong to cover shoulders and knees when away from the beach
  • Always accept mint tea when offered — it is a gesture of friendship
  • Ask permission before photographing people, especially in villages and souks
  • Dress modestly for the souk — no swimwear, cover shoulders and knees
  • Haggle with a smile — it is a social ritual, not a battle
  • Respect Ramadan: no eating, drinking, or smoking in public during daylight
  • Remove shoes when entering a home — look for shoes at the doorway as a cue
  • Use your right hand for eating, passing items, and handshakes (the left is traditionally for hygiene)

Frequently Asked Questions

Who are the Berber (Amazigh) people?
The Berbers, who call themselves Amazigh (meaning "free people"), are the indigenous inhabitants of North Africa. They have lived in Morocco for at least 4,000 years, long before the arrival of Arabs, Romans, or French. Today, Berbers make up roughly 40 to 60 per cent of Morocco's population, with a particularly strong presence in the Atlas Mountains and the Atlantic coastal regions including Taghazout, Tamraght, and Imsouane.
What language do Berbers speak?
The Berber language is called Tamazight (or Tachelhit in the Souss region around Taghazout). It has been an official language of Morocco since 2011, alongside Arabic. Many Berbers also speak Darija (Moroccan Arabic) and increasingly French. In surf villages, you will commonly hear a mix of Tachelhit, Darija, French, and English.
What is Berber hospitality like?
Berber hospitality is legendary and deeply rooted in tradition. Guests are considered a blessing, and sharing food — especially mint tea — is a sacred ritual. If you are invited into a Berber home, you will almost certainly be offered tea at minimum, and often a full meal. Refusing is considered impolite. The tradition stems from the harsh mountain and desert environments where hospitality could mean survival.
What is a Moroccan souk?
A souk is a traditional open-air market held weekly in almost every Moroccan town and village. It is far more than shopping — the souk is the social heart of the community, where people gather to trade goods, share news, and maintain social bonds. In the Taghazout region, the weekly souk in Aourir (Banana Village) every Tuesday is the largest and most vibrant.
How should I dress in Berber villages as a surf traveller?
While beach towns like Taghazout are more relaxed, modesty is appreciated, especially in smaller villages and away from the beach. Women should cover shoulders and knees in markets and villages. Men should avoid going shirtless outside of the beach. In the water, standard surf attire is fine and accepted. Carrying a light scarf or sarong to cover up when leaving the beach is a practical and respectful habit.
What does "Amazigh" mean?
Amazigh (plural: Imazighen) means "free people" or "noble people" in the Tamazight language. It is the term Berbers use to refer to themselves. The word "Berber" comes from the Greek "barbaros" (barbarian) and was imposed by outsiders — while still widely used internationally, many Moroccans prefer "Amazigh" as it is their own term for themselves.
What is the Berber New Year (Yennayer)?
Yennayer is the Amazigh New Year, celebrated on January 13. It marks the start of the agricultural year and is celebrated with special meals (usually couscous with seven vegetables), music, and family gatherings. In 2024, Morocco officially recognised Yennayer as a national public holiday for the first time.
Are there Berber symbols I should recognise?
The Amazigh flag — three horizontal stripes of blue, green, and yellow with a red Tifinagh letter "yaz" (a figure representing a free man) in the centre — is widely displayed in Berber regions. You will also see Tifinagh script (the ancient Berber alphabet) on signs, jewellery, and carpets throughout the Souss region.
How does Berber culture shape the surf villages?
Taghazout, Tamraght, Imsouane, and the surrounding villages were Berber fishing and farming communities long before surfing arrived. The surf culture that has developed here is layered on top of — and heavily influenced by — Berber traditions of hospitality, communal living, and connection to the land and sea. Many surf camp owners and guides are Berber locals whose families have lived on this coast for generations.
Is it OK to take photos of Berber people?
Always ask permission before photographing people, especially in rural villages and at the souk. Some people, particularly older generations, may be uncomfortable being photographed. A smile and a simple gesture toward your camera while making eye contact is usually enough to check. If someone declines, respect it. This is basic courtesy anywhere, but especially important in traditional communities.
