Essential Moroccan Dishes Every Surfer Should Try

Tagine

Tagine is the undisputed star of Moroccan cuisine. Named after the conical clay pot it is cooked in, tagine is a slow-cooked stew that combines meat (chicken, lamb, or beef), vegetables, dried fruits, and aromatic spices. The conical lid traps steam and returns moisture to the dish, creating incredibly tender meat and richly flavoured sauce. The most common varieties you will encounter as a surfer: chicken tagine with preserved lemon and green olives (the classic), lamb tagine with prunes and almonds (sweet and savoury), and kefta (meatball) tagine with eggs poached in tomato sauce. A good tagine with fresh bread is the ultimate post-surf meal — it replenishes protein, carbohydrates, and fluids all at once.

Couscous

Couscous is steamed semolina wheat grains served with a rich stew of vegetables (traditionally seven: carrots, courgette, pumpkin, turnip, chickpeas, cabbage, and onions) and meat. It is traditionally eaten on Fridays — the Muslim holy day — when families gather for a large midday meal. If you are in Morocco on a Friday, you will smell couscous cooking in every neighbourhood. Eating couscous with your hands, using a piece of bread to scoop, is entirely traditional and acceptable.

Msemen

Msemen (also called rghaif) is a layered, pan-fried flatbread that is simultaneously crispy, chewy, and buttery. It is typically eaten for breakfast with honey, butter, or cheese. For surfers, msemen with honey is the ideal pre-surf breakfast: light enough not to sit heavily in your stomach, but substantial enough to fuel a two-hour morning session. Most surf camps serve msemen at least a few times per week at breakfast.

Harira

Harira is a hearty tomato-based soup with lentils, chickpeas, and sometimes small pieces of lamb or beef. It is traditionally eaten to break the fast during Ramadan, but it is available year-round. For surfers, a small bowl of harira makes an excellent light breakfast before dawn patrol — warm, nourishing, and easily digestible.

Fresh Fish

The Atlantic coast provides an abundance of fresh seafood. In Taghazout, the small fishing port at the southern end of town is where local fishermen bring in their daily catch. Grilled sardines, sea bass, and dorade are common, often served simply with lemon, salt, cumin, and fresh bread. After a long surf session, nothing beats grilled fish eaten with your fingers while looking at the ocean that produced both the waves and the meal.

Pastilla

Pastilla (or bastilla) is a festive dish — layers of thin warqa pastry encasing shredded pigeon or chicken, almonds, eggs, and spices, dusted with cinnamon and powdered sugar. The combination of savoury meat, crunchy almonds, and sweet cinnamon is uniquely Moroccan. Pastilla is typically served at celebrations, but some restaurants offer it on their regular menus. It is worth seeking out.

Did You Know?

Moroccan cuisine is considered one of the most diverse in the world, influenced by Berber, Arab, Andalusian, Mediterranean, and sub-Saharan African culinary traditions. The spice combinations — cumin, cinnamon, turmeric, ginger, paprika, saffron — are what give Moroccan food its distinctive, aromatic character.

What to Eat Before Surfing

The golden rule for pre-surf eating in Morocco: keep it light. You want energy without feeling heavy in the water. Here is what our guides recommend:

  • Best choice: Msemen with honey and a banana. Light carbs with quick-release energy. Eat 45–60 minutes before paddling out.
  • Good choice: Fresh bread with olive oil and a small glass of orange juice. Simple, easily digestible, energising.
  • Liquid option: A small bowl of harira (soup). Warm, hydrating, light on the stomach.
  • Avoid: Heavy tagine, greasy fried foods, large portions of couscous, or anything that sits heavily in your stomach. Save those for after your session.

Pre-surf breakfast at a Taghazout surf camp — msemen, fresh bread, honey, olive oil, fruit, and mint tea

Best Post-Surf Meals

After two to three hours in the Atlantic, your body needs protein for muscle repair, carbohydrates for energy replenishment, and fluids for rehydration. Moroccan cuisine delivers all three in spades:

  • Chicken tagine with preserved lemon and olives: The ultimate post-surf meal. Protein from the chicken, energy from the bread you will inevitably use to mop up the sauce, and fluids from the vegetables. The preserved lemon adds a distinctive tang that cuts through any post-surf fatigue.
  • Grilled fish with Moroccan salad: Lean protein from fresh Atlantic fish, plus a chopped salad of tomatoes, cucumber, onion, and parsley dressed with olive oil and lemon. Light, refreshing, and perfectly suited to the coastal setting.
  • Vegetable couscous with chickpeas: On Fridays, this is what you will be eating. The combination of steamed semolina, slow-cooked vegetables, and chickpeas provides sustained energy for afternoon activities (or a second surf session).
Pro Tip from Our Guides

Order your tagine before you paddle out. At a local café, tell them you want a chicken tagine at 11 AM and it will be ready, steaming hot, when you walk up from the beach. The best tagines are cooked slowly and benefit from advance notice.

Where to Eat in Taghazout, Tamraght & Imsouane

Taghazout

The main street of Taghazout is lined with cafés and small restaurants catering to the surf crowd. Café Mouja (overlooking the beach at the north end) is a long-standing favourite for post-surf tagines, salads, and smoothies. L'Auberge (central Taghazout) serves reliable Moroccan classics in a relaxed rooftop setting. The small fishing port at the south end of town is where to go for the freshest grilled fish — look for the small stalls where fishermen cook their catch on charcoal grills right next to the boats.

Tamraght

Tamraght is quieter and more local than Taghazout, and the food reflects this. Family-run cafés near the beach serve authentic home-cooked tagines at prices significantly lower than the more tourist-oriented options in Taghazout. Ask your surf guide for their favourite — there are hidden gems up the side streets that most visitors never find.

Imsouane

Imsouane's food scene centres on the harbour area, where fresh fish is the star attraction. Several laid-back restaurants with ocean views serve grilled catch of the day with panoramic views of The Bay and Cathedral. After a long session at Africa's longest wave, a grilled fish lunch in Imsouane is one of life's great pleasures.

Vegetarian & Vegan Options

Morocco is surprisingly vegetarian-friendly, and with a little communication, vegan options are readily available. Essential vegetarian/vegan dishes include: vegetable tagine (specify "no meat" — "bla lhem" in Darija), vegetable couscous on Fridays, zaalouk (a smoky aubergine and tomato dip served with bread), bissara (dried broad bean soup drizzled with olive oil and cumin), taktouka (a salad of roasted peppers and tomatoes), and fresh Moroccan salads. Most surf camps cater to vegetarian and vegan dietary requirements — let them know when booking and they will adjust the menu accordingly.

Local Knowledge

In the Taghazout area, look for "Amazigh salad" on menus — a simple dish of fresh grilled peppers and tomatoes, peeled and dressed with olive oil, cumin, and salt. It is naturally vegan, incredibly flavourful, and pairs perfectly with bread as a light post-surf snack. Many cafés make it on request even if it is not on the written menu.

Street Food Guide

Moroccan street food is some of the best food you will eat, and it is generally safe if you follow a few basic rules. Look for stalls with high turnover — lots of locals eating means the food is fresh and the reputation is solid. Prefer food that is cooked fresh in front of you over pre-prepared items that have been sitting. In the coastal surf towns, you will find: grilled corn on the cob (sold by beach vendors in summer), freshly squeezed orange juice at roadside stalls, chebakia (sesame cookies soaked in honey — a Ramadan speciality), briouats (fried pastry triangles filled with meat, cheese, or vegetables), and snails in spiced broth (a popular street snack in the evening).

Drinks — Tea, Juice & Hydration

Mint tea (atay) is the universal drink of Morocco, and you will be offered it constantly. After surfing, the warm tea helps restore your core temperature and the sugar provides quick energy. Fresh orange juice is available everywhere and is exceptionally good — Morocco grows some of the world's best oranges. For hydration, bottled water is recommended (Sidi Ali, Ciel d'Afrique, and Ain Saiss are common brands). Most surf camps provide filtered drinking water for guests. Avoid tap water for drinking and brushing teeth.

What to Expect from Surf Camp Meals

Most surf camps in the Taghazout area include all meals in their packages, and the food is typically one of the highlights of the trip. Breakfast is usually served around 7:00–8:00 AM and includes msemen, fresh bread, olive oil, honey, jam, fruit, coffee, and mint tea. Lunch (the main post-surf meal) is served around 12:00–1:00 PM and features a rotating menu of tagines, couscous (Fridays), grilled fish, and salads. Dinner is lighter — often soup and bread, or leftovers from lunch. Food is served family-style at long tables, which is a great way to bond with fellow surfers from around the world.

"After a dawn patrol session in January, when the water is 16 degrees and your fingers are numb, walking into the dining room and smelling chicken tagine with preserved lemon — there is no better feeling on Earth."

— Rachid, SurfAgencyMorocco chef, Taghazout

A Day of Eating for Surfers

06:30

Wake up. Small glass of orange juice or water. A banana for quick energy.

07:00

Pre-surf light breakfast: msemen with honey, fresh bread with olive oil, mint tea. Eat, digest 30–45 minutes, then paddle out.

09:00–10:30

Morning surf session. Stay hydrated — water bottle in the van for between sets.

11:00–12:00

Post-surf: mint tea and a snack at a café overlooking the break. Rehydrate, warm up, debrief the session.

13:00

Main meal: chicken or vegetable tagine with fresh bread and salads. The recovery meal. Eat well, rest, nap.

19:00

Dinner at the surf camp: soup, bread, fruit. Light and early — tomorrow is another dawn patrol.

Surfer’s Food Checklist

  • Try a chicken tagine with preserved lemon and olives — the classic post-surf meal
  • Eat couscous on Friday — it is the traditional day and the best version you will have
  • Start your day with msemen and honey — light carbs for the dawn patrol
  • Drink mint tea after every surf session — it is the local ritual for a reason
  • Eat grilled fish at the Taghazout fishing port — straight from the boat
  • Stay hydrated — bottled or filtered water only, especially in summer
  • Try street food from busy stalls with high turnover — snails, briouats, chebakia
  • Bring any specialist dietary supplements or protein bars from home
  • Ask your surf camp about dietary requirements when booking

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I eat before surfing in Morocco?
Eat light before surfing. Msemen (Moroccan flatbread) with honey, a banana, or a small bowl of harira (soup) are ideal. Avoid heavy tagines or couscous before paddling out — they are best enjoyed after your session. Leave at least 45 to 60 minutes between eating and surfing.
What is the best post-surf meal in Morocco?
A chicken or vegetable tagine with bread is the classic post-surf meal — it provides protein, carbohydrates, and hydration. Fresh grilled fish from the port in Taghazout is another excellent option. Follow it with mint tea and fresh fruit. Most surf camps serve a generous lunch after the morning session.
Is Moroccan food suitable for vegetarians and vegans?
Yes. Vegetable tagine, vegetable couscous (on Fridays), zaalouk (smoky aubergine dip), lentil soup, bissara (bean soup), and fresh salads are widely available. Many surf camps cater to vegetarian and vegan dietary requirements — just let them know when booking.
What is tagine?
Tagine is Morocco's national dish, named after the conical clay pot in which it is cooked. It is a slow-cooked stew combining meat (chicken, lamb, or beef), vegetables, dried fruits, and aromatic spices. The conical lid traps steam, keeping the ingredients tender and moist. Common varieties include chicken with preserved lemon and olives, lamb with prunes and almonds, and kefta (meatball) tagine with eggs.
Where should I eat in Taghazout?
Café Mouja and L'Auberge are long-standing favourites for post-surf meals in Taghazout centre. For fresh fish, walk to the small fishing port at the south end of town. In Tamraght, family-run cafés near the beach serve authentic home-cooked tagines at excellent prices. Most surf camps include all meals, which is often the best and most convenient option.
Is it safe to eat street food in Morocco?
Street food is generally safe and is some of the best food you will eat in Morocco. Look for stalls with high turnover (lots of locals eating there) and food that is cooked fresh in front of you. Avoid pre-prepared items that have been sitting out. Bottled water is recommended for drinking and brushing teeth. Most surf camps use filtered water for cooking.
What is msemen?
Msemen (also called rghaif) is a layered, pan-fried Moroccan flatbread — slightly chewy, slightly crispy, and utterly delicious. It is typically served with honey, butter, or cheese for breakfast. For surfers, msemen with honey is the perfect pre-surf breakfast: light enough not to weigh you down, but with enough carbohydrates to fuel a long session.
What is Moroccan mint tea?
Moroccan mint tea (atay) is the national drink — a blend of Chinese green tea, fresh mint, and generous amounts of sugar. It is served throughout the day and is the universal symbol of hospitality. For surfers, it is the perfect post-session warmer, rehydrating and replacing energy after hours in the water.
What is couscous and when is it eaten?
Couscous is steamed semolina wheat served with a stew of vegetables and meat. It is traditionally eaten on Fridays — the holy day — when families gather for a large midday meal. Many restaurants and surf camps serve couscous on Fridays as a cultural tradition. Eating couscous with your hands (using bread to scoop) is traditional and entirely acceptable.
What is pastilla?
Pastilla (or bastilla) is a sweet-savoury pie that combines shredded pigeon or chicken with almonds, eggs, and spices, all wrapped in layers of thin warqa pastry and dusted with cinnamon and powdered sugar. It is a festive dish, traditionally served at weddings and celebrations. The combination of meat, sweetness, and crispy pastry is unlike anything else in Moroccan cuisine.
Can I drink alcohol in Taghazout?
Taghazout and the surrounding villages are predominantly Muslim and conservative — most cafés and restaurants do not serve alcohol. Some larger hotels in Agadir have licensed bars. Alcohol is available for purchase at licensed supermarkets in Agadir. If alcohol is important to your trip, check with your surf camp about their policy.
