Why Customs Matter for Surf Travellers

Morocco is a deeply welcoming country, but it operates on a different cultural wavelength than most European and North American visitors are accustomed to. Islam and Berber traditions shape everything from how people greet each other to what they wear, how they eat, and when they pray. Understanding and respecting these customs is not just about avoiding offence — it is about opening doors. A few words of Darija, appropriate dress away from the beach, and basic courtesy will transform your interactions with locals from transactional to genuinely warm.

The surf villages of Taghazout and Tamraght occupy a unique cultural space. They are more relaxed and internationally exposed than inland Moroccan towns, but they are still conservative Muslim communities at their core. The key is context awareness: what is fine on the beach or in a surf café may not be appropriate in the village centre, at the souk, or near a mosque. This guide will help you navigate those boundaries with confidence.

Did You Know?

Morocco's official religion is Islam, and the King of Morocco holds the title "Amir al-Mu'minin" (Commander of the Faithful). Islam in Morocco is characterised by the Maliki school of Sunni jurisprudence and is generally moderate and tolerant. The constitution guarantees freedom of worship, and Morocco has a long history of interfaith coexistence.

Greetings & Social Interactions

Greeting people properly is the single most important social custom in Morocco. "Salam alaykum" (peace be upon you) is the universal greeting, and you should use it whenever you enter a shop, café, or begin any interaction. The response is "Wa alaykum salam" (and peace be upon you). Using this greeting — even if you speak no other Arabic — signals respect and cultural awareness.

Handshakes are standard between men, but the dynamic changes with gender. Between a man and a woman, do not extend your hand first — wait for the woman to do so. Many Moroccan women, particularly those who are more religiously observant, prefer not to shake hands with men. If a woman places her hand over her heart and nods, reciprocate the gesture — it means the same thing and is perfectly respectful.

Always use your right hand for handshakes, passing items, giving money, and eating. The left hand is traditionally associated with personal hygiene and is considered impolite for social interactions. This is a deeply ingrained distinction that applies universally — if you hand someone money with your left hand, they may politely refuse to take it.

Dress Code — What to Wear Where

Morocco is not as conservative as some Middle Eastern countries, but modesty in dress is appreciated. The key is context:

  • On the beach: Standard surf attire is completely fine — boardshorts, bikinis, rash vests. Everyone wears the same thing in the water.
  • In surf cafés and camps: Relaxed but covered. A t-shirt or light cover-up over swimwear when sitting down to eat is appreciated. Most surf camps are casual environments where shorts and t-shirts are standard.
  • In town, villages, and the souk: More conservative. Both men and women should cover shoulders and knees. Women do not need to cover their hair — that is a personal religious choice, not a requirement for visitors. A light scarf or sarong is an incredibly useful item for women: throw it over your shoulders when entering a shop, wrap it around your waist to cover shorts, or use it to cover your head if entering a particularly conservative area.
  • For men: Avoid going shirtless anywhere except the beach. Long shorts or lightweight trousers and a t-shirt are fine for most situations.
Practical Tip

Carry a light scarf (available at any souk for a few euros) in your day bag. It serves as a cover-up, sun protection, a towel in a pinch, and a sign to locals that you are making an effort to respect their culture. One of the most useful and appreciated items a surf traveller can carry in Morocco.

Photography Etiquette

Morocco is extraordinarily photogenic — the light, the landscapes, the colours of the souk, the faces of people whose features carry millennia of Amazigh heritage. It is tempting to photograph everything. But photography of people requires consent, and this is not negotiable. Always ask before photographing someone. A simple smile and a gesture toward your camera while making eye contact is usually enough. If they smile back or nod, go ahead. If they shake their head or look away, put the camera down and move on with a smile. Never photograph people from a distance with a long lens without permission — this feels invasive and will damage trust.

Some people, particularly older women in traditional dress, almost never want to be photographed. Respect this. Photography of military installations, airports, police stations, and government buildings is strictly prohibited. The rule of thumb: if you would not photograph a stranger in your home country without asking, do not do it here either.

Haggling in the Souk

Haggling is a cultural institution in Morocco, not a sign of disrespect — it is the expected way of doing business in the souk. If you pay the first price, you will not offend anyone, but you will almost certainly overpay significantly, and you will miss out on one of the most enjoyable social rituals of Moroccan life. Here is how it works: the seller names a price — this is an opening offer, not the actual price. You counter with about half that amount, delivered with a smile. The negotiation proceeds back and forth until you meet somewhere in the middle. If you cannot agree, walking away is a legitimate tactic — if the seller calls you back, you know you are near the real price. If they do not, the price was not meant to go lower.

Key rules: keep it light and good-humoured — it is a game, not a confrontation. Know when to stop — if you have negotiated down to a price you are happy with, pay it. Do not haggle over a few dirhams (cents). And never haggle for food, drinks, or items with clearly displayed fixed prices. The souk is for haggling; the grocery store is not.

Ramadan — What to Expect

Ramadan is the holiest month in the Islamic calendar, and experiencing it in Morocco is unforgettable. During Ramadan, Muslims fast from dawn (Fajr prayer) to sunset (Maghrib prayer) — no food, no water, no smoking. The fast is broken each evening with a meal called iftar, traditionally starting with dates, milk, and harira soup, followed by a larger meal. Streets that are quiet during the day erupt with life after sunset — families gather, cafés fill, and the atmosphere is festive and communal.

For visiting surfers, Ramadan requires some adjustments. Avoid eating, drinking, or smoking in public during daylight hours out of respect — this includes water bottles. You can eat and drink in private (your accommodation, your surf camp's dining area), but be discreet. Many cafés and restaurants are closed or operate reduced hours during the day. Surf camps continue to operate normally and provide meals for guests — you will not go hungry. Surfing is fine, though you may find that your local guides have less energy in the afternoon than usual (they are not eating or drinking). Be understanding — it is a physically demanding month.

Ramadan Alert

Ramadan dates shift approximately 10 days earlier each Gregorian year. In 2026, Ramadan is expected to begin around February 18. In 2027, around February 8. Check the Islamic calendar for your intended travel dates. The month culminates in Eid al-Fitr, a three-day celebration with feasting and family gatherings — a wonderful time to be in Morocco if you plan for it.

Friday Prayer & Weekly Rhythm

Friday is the Muslim holy day, and the midday prayer (Dhuhr, around 12:30–1:30 PM) is the most important of the week for men to attend at the mosque. You will notice this — streets around mosques fill with worshippers, many shops close for one to two hours, and the atmosphere shifts from commercial to spiritual. Plan your shopping and errands around this window. Surfing continues as normal — the waves do not observe the holy day. Friday is also the traditional day for couscous — families gather for a large midday meal after prayer. Many surf camps serve couscous on Fridays as a cultural tradition, and it is often the best meal of the week.

Tipping Guide

Tipping (bakshish) is customary in Morocco and is an important part of service workers' income. Here is a practical guide:

  • Restaurants: 5–10% for good service, if a service charge is not already included.
  • Café: Round up to the nearest 5 or 10 dirhams, or leave 2–5 dirhams for tea or coffee.
  • Taxi: Round up the fare. For longer journeys, an extra 10–20 dirhams is appreciated.
  • Hotel/riad staff: 20–50 dirhams for porters and housekeeping at the end of your stay.
  • Surf guides: It is common to tip your guide at the end of your stay. 50–100 dirhams per day of guiding is generous and appreciated. If you have had an exceptional experience, more is always welcome.
  • Parking attendants and informal helpers: 2–5 dirhams is standard for small services.

Alcohol — What You Need to Know

Morocco is a Muslim country where alcohol is culturally sensitive. In Taghazout, Tamraght, and Imsouane, most cafés and restaurants do not serve alcohol — these are conservative communities where public drinking is not part of the culture. Some larger hotels and resorts in Agadir (15–20 minutes by car) have licensed bars. Alcohol can be purchased at licensed supermarkets in Agadir (Carrefour, Marjane) during limited hours — look for the separate alcohol section, usually with its own entrance. If you are staying at a surf camp, check their policy: most are alcohol-free or permit discreet consumption in private areas. Public drunkenness is absolutely unacceptable and will damage the reputation of both you and the surf community. If alcohol is an essential part of your holiday, plan accordingly — but know that many surf travellers find the alcohol-free rhythm of the surf villages to be a refreshing change.

Public Displays of Affection

Morocco is conservative regarding physical intimacy in public. Holding hands is generally acceptable for married couples, though you may receive curious looks. Kissing, prolonged hugging, or overt physical affection in public is considered inappropriate — even between married couples. In the surf villages, attitudes are somewhat more relaxed due to international exposure, but discretion is still appreciated. The guideline is simple: if you imagine your grandmother watching, you are probably fine. Anything more intimate should be reserved for private settings.

Advice for Women Travellers

Morocco is generally safe for women travelling alone, though street harassment (catcalling, unwanted attention) can be an issue, particularly in larger cities. The surf villages are notably more relaxed — Taghazout and Tamraght have large international communities, and women surfers are a common sight. Dressing modestly reduces unwanted attention. Walking with confidence, avoiding eye contact with persistent individuals, and ignoring harassment rather than engaging are the most effective strategies. Booking through a reputable surf camp provides a supportive network, local contacts, and the safety of a group environment. Many women surf travellers report feeling safer and more welcomed in Morocco's surf communities than they expected — the key is preparation and cultural awareness.

Etiquette Checklist

  • Greet everyone with "Salam alaykum" — shopkeepers, guides, café staff
  • Use your right hand for handshakes, passing money, and eating
  • Cover shoulders and knees away from the beach — carry a scarf
  • Ask permission before photographing people — always
  • During Ramadan, avoid eating, drinking, or smoking in public during daylight
  • Tip guides, drivers, and service staff — it is customary and appreciated
  • Do not enter mosques unless explicitly invited (Hassan II is the exception)
  • Avoid public displays of affection beyond hand-holding
  • Learn at least "Salam alaykum," "Shukran," and "La bas"

Frequently Asked Questions

How should I dress in Morocco as a surf traveller?
On the beach and in surf-oriented areas of Taghazout and Tamraght, standard surf attire is fine. Away from the beach — in villages, the souk, and town centres — both men and women should cover shoulders and knees. Women do not need to cover their hair, but carrying a light scarf or sarong to cover up when needed is practical and respectful. Avoid visible swimwear in town.
How do I greet people in Morocco?
"Salam alaykum" (peace be upon you) is the universal greeting, with the response "Wa alaykum salam." A handshake is standard between men. Between men and women, wait for the woman to extend her hand first. A hand over the heart with a nod and smile is a perfectly respectful alternative. Use your right hand for handshakes, passing items, and eating.
Can I take photos of people and places in Morocco?
Always ask permission before photographing people, especially in rural villages and the souk. Some people, particularly older generations and women, may not want to be photographed. Photography of military installations, airports, and government buildings is prohibited. A smile and a gesture toward your camera is usually enough to check — if someone declines, respect it without question.
How do I haggle in a Moroccan souk?
Haggling is expected and is part of the social fabric of the souk. Start at about half the initial asking price and negotiate toward the middle with a smile and good humour. It is a social interaction, not a battle. Walking away is a valid tactic — if the seller calls you back, you know you are close to the real price. Never haggle for food or fixed-price items.
What should I know about Ramadan in Morocco?
During Ramadan, Muslims fast from dawn to sunset — no food, water, or smoking. As a visitor, avoid eating, drinking, or smoking in public during daylight hours out of respect. Many cafés and restaurants are closed or have reduced hours during the day but come alive after sunset (iftar). Surf camps continue operating normally and provide meals for guests in private.
Is tipping expected in Morocco?
Yes, tipping is customary. In restaurants, tip 5 to 10 per cent for good service. For guides, taxi drivers, and service staff, round up the fare or tip 10 to 20 dirhams for good service. At surf camps, it is common to tip guides at the end of your stay — 50 to 100 dirhams per day of guiding is a generous and appreciated amount.
Can I drink alcohol in Morocco?
Alcohol is available in Morocco but is culturally sensitive. Most cafés and restaurants in Taghazout and the surf villages do not serve alcohol. Larger hotels in Agadir have licensed bars, and alcohol can be purchased at licensed supermarkets in Agadir. Public drunkenness is unacceptable. If you drink at your accommodation, be discreet and respectful of local norms. Most surf camps are alcohol-free.
What about public displays of affection in Morocco?
Morocco is a conservative country regarding public displays of affection. Holding hands is generally fine for married couples, but kissing, prolonged hugging, or overt displays of physical intimacy in public are considered inappropriate. In the surf villages, attitudes are more relaxed than in more conservative inland areas, but discretion is still appreciated.
Can I enter a mosque in Morocco?
With a few exceptions (such as the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca, which offers guided tours), non-Muslims are not permitted to enter mosques in Morocco. You can appreciate the beautiful architecture and minarets from outside. During prayer times, be respectful — avoid loud noise or disruptive behaviour near mosques, especially during the Friday midday prayer.
What is Friday prayer and how does it affect my day?
Friday is the Muslim holy day. The midday prayer (around 12:30 to 1:30 PM) is the most important of the week, and many shops and businesses close during this time. Men gather at mosques — you will see large crowds spilling onto the streets around mosques. Plan your errands and shopping around the Friday prayer window. Surfing continues as normal.
Is it safe for women travelling alone in Morocco?
Morocco is generally safe for women travelling alone, but street harassment can be an issue, especially in larger cities. Dressing modestly reduces unwanted attention. Walking with confidence, avoiding eye contact with persistent individuals, and ignoring harassment are the most effective strategies. In the surf villages, women travellers generally report feeling safe and welcomed. Booking through a reputable surf camp provides a supportive base and local contacts.
Should I learn Arabic or French for my surf trip?
A few words go a long way. 'Salam alaykum' (hello), 'Shukran' (thank you), 'La bas?' (how are you?), 'B'saha' (to your health), and 'Insha'Allah' (God willing) are all appreciated. French is widely spoken in Morocco and is the most useful second language for communicating beyond basic greetings. English is increasingly spoken in surf-related businesses but is not universal.
