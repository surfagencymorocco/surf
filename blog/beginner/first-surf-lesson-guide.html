Before the Lesson — What to Do

What to Wear

You need very little for your first surf lesson. Wear your swimsuit or board shorts to the beach. Your surf camp or school will provide a wetsuit (appropriate for the season — a 4/3mm in winter, 3/2mm in shoulder months, or a 2mm top in summer) and a large foam surfboard. Do not wear jewellery, watches, or anything that could snag on the board or wetsuit. Leave your phone and valuables in your room — they will not survive the sand and saltwater.

Sunscreen

This is critical. Apply high-SPF (50+) zinc-based sunscreen to your face, neck, ears, and the backs of your hands at least 20 minutes before the lesson. Zinc sunscreen is thick, visible, and stays on in the water — unlike chemical sunscreens that wash off in minutes. The Moroccan sun, combined with water reflection, burns faster than you think. A sunburnt face on day 1 can ruin your entire week. Reapply after every session.

Hydration

Drink water before your lesson. You will be exercising in the sun for two hours, and dehydration leads to fatigue, cramps, and headaches. Bring a reusable water bottle to the beach. Avoid heavy meals right before the lesson — eat a light breakfast (fruit, bread, yoghurt) about an hour before.

Coaching Tip

Stretch for 5 minutes before your lesson — focus on your shoulders, back, hips, and hamstrings. Surfing uses muscles that most people do not use in daily life. A quick warm-up reduces soreness and improves your range of motion for the pop-up.

Beach Theory — Safety, Rips & Etiquette

Your lesson begins on the sand, not in the water. This 20–30 minute briefing is the most important part of the lesson — pay attention. Here is what your instructor will cover:

Board Handling

You will learn how to carry your board (under your arm, not over your head — wind can catch it and knock you over), how to place it on the sand (fins up, deck down), and how to enter the water safely (always push the board nose-first through waves, never let the board get between you and an incoming wave).

Positioning on the Board

The most common beginner mistake is lying too far back or too far forward on the board. Your instructor will show you the correct position: your toes should be near the tail, your chest slightly lifted, and the nose of the board should be about 5–10 cm out of the water. Lie too far back and the board drags — too far forward and you will nosedive (pearl) every time.

Rip Currents

Your instructor will teach you how to identify rip currents — they look like darker, calmer channels of water flowing out between breaking waves. If you ever get caught in one: do not panic, do not paddle against it. Paddle parallel to the shore (across the rip) until you are out of the current, then paddle back to the beach. Rips are not undertows — they do not pull you under, only out. In your first lesson, you will be in a controlled, shallow area where rips are minimal to nonexistent.

Surf Etiquette

Even as a beginner, you need to know the basics: the surfer closest to the peak (the breaking part of the wave) has priority. Do not drop in on someone else's wave. If you lose your board, check behind you before surfacing — a loose board can hit other surfers. And always hold onto your board if possible; a loose board is dangerous to everyone in the water.

The Pop-Up — Practised on Sand

You will practise the pop-up movement several times on the beach before entering the water. Your instructor will correct your form — where your hands go, how your feet land, what your body position should look like. This dry-land practice is essential: the more you drill it on sand, the more automatic it becomes in the water.

The Pop-Up — Step-by-Step Breakdown

The pop-up is the move that takes you from lying on the board to standing on your feet. It is the single most important skill in surfing, and most Moroccan surf schools break it down into four simple steps:

  1. Lie centred on the board. Your body should be straight along the centre line. Toes touching the tail. Chest lifted slightly — looking forward, not down at the board. Your hands grip the rails (sides) of the board near your chest.
  2. Place your hands flat on the board at chest level. Just like the start of a push-up. Hands directly under your shoulders. Elbows tucked in, not splayed out (splayed elbows make you unstable).
  3. Push up and bring your front foot forward in one smooth motion. This is the key moment. Explode upward from your hands while simultaneously bringing your front foot to land between your hands — roughly where your chest was. Your back foot follows naturally, landing near the tail.
  4. Land in a low, athletic stance. Feet shoulder-width apart. Knees deeply bent. Arms out for balance. Eyes up, looking towards the beach. Your head is heavy — where you look, your body follows. Look down, and you will fall down. Look forward, and you will ride.
The Most Common Pop-Up Mistake

Grabbing the rails and pulling yourself up knee-by-knee (like getting up off the floor). This is slow, unstable, and will not work on a moving wave. The pop-up must be one explosive motion — hands push, feet land. If you find yourself on your knees, you are doing it wrong. Practise the correct technique on the floor at home before your trip — it makes a huge difference.

In the Water — Your First Waves

After the beach briefing, you carry your board into the water — and this is where the real fun begins. Your instructor will position you in waist-deep water in the white-water zone (where waves have already broken and are rolling towards the shore as foam).

Here is what happens: Your instructor will hold your board, watching the waves behind you. When a good wave approaches, they will say "paddle, paddle, paddle!" You paddle hard (3–5 strong strokes), feel the wave lift the tail of your board, and your instructor will shout "UP!" — that is your cue to pop up. On your first few attempts, you will probably fall almost immediately. That is expected, and even celebrated — falling means you are trying.

Within 30–60 minutes, most beginners stand up for the first time. The board glides forward on the white water, you are standing, the beach is rushing towards you, and for 5–10 seconds — you are surfing. The feeling is addictive. After that first successful ride, you will paddle back out and do it again. And again. And again.

"I have taught thousands of beginners over fifteen years. Every single one of them smiles the same way after that first wave. It is not about technique, not about style — it is about the pure joy of sliding across water. That smile never gets old for me."

— Fatima, SurfAgencyMorocco instructor, Imsouane

Common Fears — Reassured with Facts

"What about sharks?"

There are no dangerous sharks in the waters where surf lessons take place in Morocco. The Atlantic coast from Agadir to Essaouira has zero recorded shark incidents involving surfers. Shark attacks in Morocco overall are virtually nonexistent — the last confirmed incident was decades ago, well south of the surf zone, and involved a swimmer, not a surfer. In thousands of lessons taught each year along this coast, no surfer has ever encountered a dangerous shark during a lesson. You are more likely to be struck by lightning (and lightning is very rare in Morocco). The only marine life you are likely to see are small fish and maybe a dolphin in the distance.

"What if I drown?"

You will be in waist-deep water where you can stand on the sand bottom at any time. You can stop, stand up, catch your breath, and try again whenever you want. Your instructor is beside you at all times. The foam boards used in beginner lessons are highly buoyant and essentially function as flotation devices. The waves you will be riding are broken white water — they have already spent their energy breaking and are rolling gently towards shore. Drowning in a beginner surf lesson in Morocco is effectively impossible under normal circumstances.

"What if I look stupid?"

Every surfer — including Kelly Slater — looked stupid the first time they tried. Falling, splashing, getting water up your nose, losing your board, standing up for half a second and immediately face-planting — these are all part of learning. No one at the beach is judging you. The surf community is overwhelmingly supportive of beginners because every surfer remembers what it felt like to be one.

Remember

The only failed surf lesson is the one you do not take. Falling off the board is not failure — it is data. Every fall teaches you something about balance, timing, and body position. The best surfers in the world fall more times in a single session than a beginner falls in an entire week — they just get back on the board faster.

After the Lesson — What to Expect

After 90 minutes in the water, you will be tired but exhilarated. Your arms and shoulders will ache — paddling uses muscles that most people do not train. You might have a small bruise or two on your ribs from popping up on the board (this is normal — it is called "surfer's rib" and fades as your technique improves). You will be hungry, thirsty, and probably already planning your next session.

Your instructor will give you brief feedback — what you did well, what to work on tomorrow. Most camps film at least some sessions for video analysis, which is incredibly useful. Watching yourself surf reveals habits you did not know you had.

The next day, you will feel muscle soreness in your shoulders, back, and core. This is normal and fades by day 3 as your body adapts. Stretch, hydrate, eat well, and get ready for session two — this is where the real progress starts.

Timeline of a 2-Hour First Lesson

0:00–0:05

Arrive at the beach. Meet your instructor and group. Sunscreen check, wetsuit fitting, board assignment.

0:05–0:30

Beach briefing. Safety talk (rip currents, board handling, etiquette). Pop-up demonstration and dry-land practice. Instructor corrects your form individually.

0:30–1:00

Into the water. Waist-deep white-water zone. Instructor pushes you into waves. First pop-up attempts. Probably falling — probably laughing. Learning to read the waves.

1:00–1:30

Standing up. Most people get their first successful rides in this phase. Instructor gives individual feedback between waves. You are starting to feel the rhythm.

1:30–2:00

End of session. Brief feedback from instructor. Walk back to camp. Rinse off. You are officially a surfer now — welcome to the club.

Pre-Lesson Checklist

  • Apply high-SPF zinc sunscreen to face, neck, ears, and hands 20 minutes before the lesson
  • Drink water — aim for 500ml before the lesson
  • Eat a light breakfast 1 hour before (fruit, bread, yoghurt — not a heavy tagine)
  • Wear your swimsuit/board shorts to the beach (wetsuit provided)
  • Remove jewellery, watches, and leave valuables in your room
  • If you wear contact lenses, put them in (daily disposables recommended)
  • Stretch for 5 minutes — shoulders, back, hips, hamstrings
  • Bring a positive attitude — you will fall, and that is the whole point

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens in a first surf lesson?
A typical 2-hour first surf lesson has three parts: (1) 20–30 minute beach briefing covering safety, board handling, and the pop-up technique practised on sand; (2) 60–90 minutes in waist-deep water with your instructor pushing you into white-water waves; (3) a brief debrief with feedback. Most beginners stand up at least once in their first lesson.
What should I wear to a surf lesson?
Wear a swimsuit or board shorts under your wetsuit (provided by the surf camp). Apply high-SPF zinc sunscreen to your face, neck, and any exposed skin 20 minutes before the lesson. Do not wear jewellery, watches, or glasses. If you wear contact lenses, they are generally fine under the water — but bring a spare pair just in case.
Will I stand up in my first surf lesson?
Most people do — about 80% of first-timers stand up at least once during their first 2-hour lesson. However, not standing up on the first day is completely normal and not a failure. Surfing is difficult. The key is to relax, listen to your instructor, and enjoy the process. Consistency matters more than first-day results.
Are there sharks in the water during surf lessons in Morocco?
No — there are no dangerous sharks in the waters where surf lessons take place in Morocco. The Atlantic coast from Agadir to Essaouira has no recorded shark incidents involving surfers. The only marine life you are likely to encounter are small fish and the occasional dolphin in the distance. This is one of the safest surf destinations in the world in terms of marine hazards.
What if I cannot swim well?
Beginner surf lessons take place in waist-deep water where you can stand on the sand bottom at any time. You do not need to be a strong swimmer — but you do need to be comfortable in the water and able to swim at least 50 metres. If you are very nervous about the water, consider taking a few swimming lessons before your surf trip. Some camps offer lessons in even shallower (knee-deep) water for very nervous beginners.
Do I need my own surfboard for a first lesson?
No — surf camps and schools provide large, soft foam boards (8–9 ft) that are specifically designed for beginners. These boards are stable, buoyant, and have soft decks that are safe to fall on. Do not buy a surfboard before your first lesson — what you think you need and what you actually need are often very different.
How physically demanding is a first surf lesson?
It is moderately demanding. You will paddle (which uses shoulder, back, and arm muscles you probably do not use daily), pop up repeatedly (core and legs), and walk through water (resistance work). Most beginners are tired but not exhausted after a 2-hour lesson. You will probably feel muscles you did not know you had the next day — this is normal and fades by day 3.
What are rip currents and should I be worried?
Rip currents are narrow channels of water flowing out to sea. They are a natural part of how the ocean circulates and are common at surf beaches. Your instructor will teach you how to identify them (look for darker, calmer-looking water channels between breaking waves) and what to do if caught in one (paddle parallel to the shore, not against the rip). In beginner lessons, you will be in controlled, shallow areas where rip currents are minimal.
Can I wear glasses or contact lenses while surfing?
Contact lenses are fine — most surfing optometrists recommend daily disposables so you do not lose expensive reusable lenses. Soft lenses stay in place well during surfing. Glasses are not practical — they will be knocked off by waves, and prescription surf goggles exist but are rarely necessary for beginners (you do not need perfect vision to catch waist-high white water).
How many lessons do I need before I can surf on my own?
Most people feel ready to practise independently (catching white-water waves without an instructor pushing them) after 3–5 lessons. Catching green (unbroken) waves independently typically takes 1–2 weeks of daily lessons. However, surfing is a lifelong learning process — even experienced surfers take lessons and coaching to improve.
What time of day are beginner surf lessons?
Most surf camps schedule beginner lessons in the morning (8–10 AM) when the wind is lightest and the waves are cleanest, and a second session in the afternoon (3–5 PM). Morning sessions are generally better — the conditions are optimal and you have more energy. Some camps adjust lesson times daily based on tides and conditions.
What if I get water up my nose or swallow water?
This happens to everyone — including experienced surfers. You will get water in your nose, swallow some seawater, and probably cough and splutter a few times. The Atlantic off Morocco is clean (no pollution issues at surf beaches), and swallowing a mouthful of saltwater is unpleasant but harmless. You quickly learn to time your breathing around the waves.
