Why Morocco Is Great for Beginner Surfers

Morocco is not just a good place to learn to surf — it is one of the best. The Atlantic coast between Agadir and Essaouira offers a rare combination of ingredients that make learning to surf faster, safer, and more enjoyable than almost anywhere else in the world.

1. Consistent, gentle waves. The Moroccan coast receives year-round swell from the North Atlantic, but unlike the raw power of Europe's winter waves, many of Morocco's breaks are sheltered, sandy-bottomed, and beginner-friendly. The Bay at Imsouane is the crown jewel — a slow, peeling right-hander that runs for up to 800 metres over sand. There are very few waves in the world where a complete beginner can ride for 50–100 metres on their very first lesson.

2. Warm water (most of the year). Water temperatures range from 16°C in winter to 23°C in summer. This means you spend less energy shivering and more energy learning. From May to October, you can comfortably surf in a 3/2mm wetsuit or even boardshorts. In winter, a 4/3mm wetsuit keeps you warm for 2-hour sessions.

3. Hundreds of qualified instructors. The Taghazout–Imsouane corridor has one of the highest concentrations of surf instructors in Africa. Most are local Moroccans who grew up surfing these waves and hold internationally recognised certifications. They know every sandbank, every tide, and exactly where to take beginners on any given day.

4. Affordable all-inclusive surf camps. For â‚¬350–900 per week, you get accommodation, two daily surf lessons, all equipment, three meals a day, and airport transfers. This all-inclusive model removes every logistical headache so you can focus entirely on learning to surf.

5. 300+ sunny days per year. You are almost guaranteed good weather. The Moroccan sun is reliable, the rain is rare, and even in winter, daytime temperatures are a pleasant 15–22°C.

Did You Know?

More people learn to surf in Morocco each year than in any other African country. The combination of consistent waves, warm water, and structured surf camps has turned this stretch of coast into the continent's undisputed capital of beginner surfing.

What to Expect in Your First Lesson

Your first surf lesson in Morocco typically follows a proven structure. Here is exactly what will happen:

Part 1: Beach Briefing (20–30 minutes)

Before you touch the water, your instructor will cover the essentials on the beach: how to carry your board, how to lie on it correctly (not too far forward, not too far back), how to paddle, and — most importantly — how to pop up. You will practise the pop-up movement several times on the sand until it feels natural. Your instructor will also explain water safety: how to identify rip currents, what to do if you get caught in one, and basic surf etiquette.

Part 2: The Pop-Up — Step by Step

The pop-up is the single most important skill you will learn. Here is the four-step breakdown most Moroccan surf schools teach:

  1. Lie centred on the board. Your toes should touch the tail, your chest slightly lifted. Look forward, not down.
  2. Place your hands flat on the board at chest level. Like a push-up position — hands under your shoulders, elbows in.
  3. Push up and bring your front foot forward in one motion. Your front foot lands between your hands, roughly where your chest was. Your back foot follows naturally.
  4. Land in a low, athletic stance. Feet shoulder-width apart, knees bent, arms out for balance. Look where you want to go — your board follows your eyes.

Part 3: Into the Water (60–90 minutes)

You will start in waist-deep white water — waves that have already broken. Your instructor will push you into waves, telling you when to paddle and when to pop up. The first few attempts will likely end in a splash — that is completely normal and part of the fun. Within 30–60 minutes, most beginners stand up for the first time. The feeling is unforgettable.

Coaching Tip

The number one mistake beginners make is looking down at the board during the pop-up. Your head is heavy — where it goes, your body follows. Keep your eyes up, looking towards the beach. Your instructor will probably shout "LOOK UP!" at least twenty times during your first lesson. Listen to them.

How to Choose a Surf Camp for Beginners

Not all surf camps are equal. Here is what to look for when choosing a camp as a beginner:

  • Instructor-to-student ratio (max 1:5). This is the single most important factor. A ratio of 1:5 or better ensures you get individual feedback and attention. Avoid camps that pack 10+ students per instructor — you will spend more time waiting than learning.
  • Video analysis. Camps that film your sessions and review the footage with you afterwards help you improve dramatically faster. Seeing yourself surf is far more powerful than being told what you did wrong.
  • Location. Camps in Imsouane are ideal for absolute beginners — you will learn on The Bay. Camps in Tamraght are great for beginners who want access to multiple beach breaks. Camps in Taghazout are better for intermediates who want a mix of coaching and independent surfing.
  • Equipment quality. Check recent reviews for mentions of board and wetsuit condition. A camp with old, waterlogged foam boards and leaky wetsuits will make learning harder than it needs to be.
  • What is included. "All-inclusive" means different things to different camps. Specifically confirm: are lunches included? Is equipment rental covered for the entire stay (not just lesson time)? Are airport transfers both ways?
  • Recent reviews. Look for reviews from 2025–2026. Camps change ownership, instructors leave, and quality can shift dramatically from one season to the next.
Guides' Advice

Before You Go — Preparation

  • Swimming. You should be able to swim at least 50 metres unassisted and be comfortable putting your head underwater. If you are not, take a few swimming lessons before your trip — it will make the experience far more enjoyable.
  • Basic fitness. Surfing is physically demanding. Paddling uses muscles you probably do not use in daily life. A few weeks of swimming, push-ups, and core exercises before your trip will make your first days much easier.
  • Flexibility. The pop-up requires hip and shoulder mobility. Basic stretching, yoga, or simply practising the pop-up movement on the floor at home will help.
  • Sunscreen. Bring high-SPF zinc sunscreen that will not wash off in the water. The Moroccan sun, combined with water reflection, burns beginners faster than they expect. A sunburnt back on day 2 ruins the rest of the week.
  • Hydration. You will be exercising in the sun for 3–4 hours a day. Bring a large reusable water bottle and drink more than you think you need to.

Timeline of a Typical Beginner Surf Camp Day

07:30

Breakfast at the camp. Moroccan pancakes, fresh fruit, orange juice, coffee, mint tea. Fuel up — you will burn it off in the water.

08:00

Morning surf lesson. The best conditions of the day — offshore wind, clean waves. Your instructor checks conditions and chooses the optimal spot for your level.

10:00

Lesson ends. You are tired, happy, and probably covered in sand. Walk back to camp, rinse off, relax on the rooftop terrace.

12:30

Lunch. Traditional Moroccan food — tagine, couscous, grilled fish, fresh salads. Rest and digest.

15:00

Afternoon surf session. Second lesson or free practice. The wind may be slightly onshore now, but the waves are still fun and you are putting the morning's learning into practice.

17:00

Video analysis (if your camp offers it). Watching yourself surf is humbling and incredibly useful. Your instructor points out what you did well and what to work on tomorrow.

19:00

Dinner. More Moroccan cuisine. Swap stories with other surfers on the terrace. Watch the sunset over the Atlantic. You will sleep well tonight.

Beginner's Packing Checklist

  • 2–3 swimsuits/board shorts (you will be in the water twice a day)
  • High-SPF zinc sunscreen (SPF 50+, water-resistant, reef-safe)
  • Reusable water bottle (1.5–2L)
  • Wetsuit (if you own one — otherwise your camp provides it)
  • Rash vest or lycra top (extra sun protection under your wetsuit)
  • Flip-flops or sandals (for the walk to the beach)
  • Hat and sunglasses (for when you are not surfing)
  • Lightweight long-sleeve shirts (sun protection between sessions)
  • Warm hoodie or jacket (evenings can be cool, even in summer)
  • Microfibre towel (dries fast, takes little space)
  • Cash in Moroccan Dirhams (for tips, snacks, and small purchases)
  • Basic fitness — practise pop-ups at home for 2–3 weeks before your trip

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Morocco a good place to learn to surf?
Morocco offers warm water, consistent gentle waves, hundreds of qualified instructors, and affordable all-inclusive surf camps. The Atlantic coast has sandy beach breaks perfect for beginners, and destinations like Imsouane Bay offer waves so long and forgiving that first-timers routinely ride 50–100 metres on their first lesson.
How long does it take to learn to surf?
Most beginners stand up on a wave (white water) within their first 1–2 hour lesson. Riding a green (unbroken) wave typically takes 2–5 days of daily lessons. Feeling comfortable catching waves independently usually takes 1–2 weeks of consistent surfing. Everyone progresses at their own pace.
What is the best surf spot for beginners in Morocco?
The Bay at Imsouane is widely considered the best beginner wave in Morocco. It breaks slowly over a sand bottom with no rocks or reef, produces rides up to 800 metres, and has an easy paddle-out. Tamraght's beach breaks (Banana Beach, Crocodile Beach) are also excellent for beginners with multiple gentle peaks.
Should I book a surf camp or take individual lessons?
For beginners, an all-inclusive surf camp is strongly recommended. It bundles accommodation, daily lessons, equipment, meals, and transfers into one price — usually cheaper than booking everything separately. Camps also provide a structured learning environment and a social atmosphere that makes the experience more enjoyable.
What should I bring for my first surf trip to Morocco?
A wetsuit (if you own one, otherwise camps provide them), multiple swimsuits, high-SPF zinc sunscreen, a reusable water bottle, a hat, sunglasses, flip-flops, warm clothes for evenings (winter), and cash in Moroccan Dirhams. Most surf camps provide boards and wetsuits, so you do not need to bring your own equipment as a beginner.
Do I need to be a strong swimmer to learn to surf?
You should be comfortable in the water and able to swim at least 50 metres unassisted. Beginner surf lessons take place in waist-deep water where you can stand, so you do not need to be an Olympic swimmer. However, basic water confidence is essential — if you are not comfortable in the ocean, start with swimming lessons first.

What if I cannot stand up on the first day?
Do not worry — many people do not stand up on their very first day, and that is completely normal. Surfing is difficult, and everyone learns at their own pace. A good instructor will break down the pop-up into simple steps, give you feedback, and celebrate every small win. By day 2 or 3, most people are riding waves consistently.
Is Morocco safe for beginner surfers?
Yes — Morocco is one of the safest places to learn to surf. The beginner breaks (Imsouane Bay, Tamraght) have sand bottoms with no dangerous marine life. Qualified instructors teach water safety, rip current awareness, and surf etiquette. The surf community is welcoming, and the main hazard is the sun — wear sunscreen.
What is the best age to learn to surf?
There is no 'best' age — people learn to surf from ages 5 to 65+. Children as young as 5–6 can start with dedicated kids' lessons using small foam boards. Adults in their 50s and 60s learn regularly. The key factors are reasonable fitness, water confidence, and a good instructor — not age.
When is the best time for beginners to surf in Morocco?
September–November and March–May are ideal for beginners. These shoulder seasons offer warm water (19–22°C), smaller, more manageable waves (knee-to-waist-high), fewer crowds than winter, and moderate prices. Summer (June–August) is also good for beginners with warm water but less consistent swell.
Can I learn to surf if I am not fit?
You do not need to be an athlete to learn to surf, but a basic level of fitness helps. Beginner lessons involve paddling (which is tiring at first), popping up repeatedly, and walking through water. Any reasonably active person can handle a beginner lesson. The more you surf, the fitter you will become — it is excellent exercise.
