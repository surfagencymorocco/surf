Anchor Point on Big Days

On small-to-medium days, Anchor Point is accessible to confident intermediates. When the swell hits 6 ft+ with a 14-second period or longer, it transforms into an entirely different beast. The take-off becomes steep, critical, and late. The reef shelf that sits harmlessly a metre below the surface on small days is suddenly a real hazard. The current that runs along the point doubles in strength. Two-wave hold-downs become a genuine possibility.

What changes at size: The wave jacks up more abruptly on the take-off, requiring a later, more committed drop. The sections connect differently — at 6–8 ft, the mid-section and inside often link into one long, racing wall. Barrel sections appear that do not exist on smaller days. The crowd thins out because most surfers simply cannot handle it.

Skill needed: You must be comfortable taking late drops on steep faces over shallow reef. You must be able to duck dive a 6'4" shortboard under a 6 ft wall of white water. You must be fit enough to paddle against the current for a two-hour session. If any of these three statements makes you hesitate, Anchor Point on a big day is not your wave — yet.

Important

On big days at Anchor Point, the rip current accelerates dramatically. If you get caught in it, do not fight it head-on. Paddle at a 45-degree angle toward the beach, then use the channel on the inside to make your way back to the peak. Fighting the rip directly is how surfers exhaust themselves and get into trouble.

Killer Point — The Underrated Heavyweight

Killer Point has a reputation as Anchor Point’s mellower sibling, and on small days, that reputation is fair. But when the swell hits 6–8 ft, Killer Point reveals its teeth. The wave jacks up over a rock shelf on the take-off, producing a steep, fast drop. The mid-section walls up beautifully for carving, but the inside section funnels into a shallow area where the wave can double up and pitch.

What makes it advanced at size: The combination of a rocky take-off, a strong lateral current, and the inside double-up section. On a big day at Killer Point, you cannot afford to fall on the take-off — the rocks are right there. The inside reform can catch you by surprise if you kick out too late.

Best conditions: NW swell 5–8 ft at 12–16 seconds, mid-tide rising. On these days, Killer Point offers one of the longest, most satisfying rides on the Taghazout coast with a fraction of Anchor Point’s crowd.

Draculas — Morocco’s Most Dangerous Wave

Draculas is not a wave for the faint-hearted or the unprepared. Located just north of Taghazout, this right-hand slab breaks over a barely submerged rock ledge that, at low tide, can be centimetres below the surface. It only works on specific swell and tide combinations, and when it does, it produces a short, intense, hollow barrel that demands absolute commitment.

Hazards: The reef is the obvious one. A wipeout at Draculas can mean contact with rock, sea urchins, or both. The take-off is late and steep. There is no channel — you paddle straight back out through the impact zone. The wave holds you down longer than a beach break because it breaks in shallow water. Booties are non-negotiable. A helmet is not a bad idea.

Who should surf it: Expert surfers only. You need to be completely comfortable with late drops over shallow reef, hold-downs, and paddling through a heavy impact zone without a channel. If you are visiting Morocco and want to test yourself, go with a guide who knows the wave — Draculas punishes mistakes harshly.

Local Knowledge

Draculas only breaks on a handful of days each winter. It needs a very specific NW swell direction (310°–330°), a mid-to-high tide, and ideally a light offshore wind. Many local surfers surf it fewer than 10 times a year. When it is on, you will see a handful of the best locals on it — that is your sign that conditions are right.

Boilers — Heavy Right-Hand Reef

Boilers is named after the churning, boiling water that appears over the reef when a set approaches. Located about 20 minutes north of Taghazout, this right-hand reef break is less fickle than Draculas but no less serious. It produces powerful, hollow waves that break over a mix of reef and volcanic rock, with a take-off that demands precision and a barrel section that makes the risk worthwhile.

Conditions: Boilers needs a solid NW swell (4–8 ft) and a mid-to-high tide. At low tide, the reef is dangerously exposed and the wave becomes almost unsurfable. On a rising mid-tide with clean conditions, Boilers offers some of the heaviest, most exhilarating waves on the Moroccan coast.

What to know: Bring booties. Bring a board you are not emotionally attached to (boards get dinged here). Know the exit — there is a channel on the inside, but it requires navigating around a rocky outcrop. A local guide who knows the reef layout is invaluable for your first session here.

Tamri Reefs — Empty Lineups, Heavy Waves

The stretch of coast around Tamri, roughly 30 minutes north of Taghazout, is the least explored surf zone in the area. Unlike the well-documented breaks of Taghazout Bay, Tamri’s reefs require exploration, local knowledge, and a willingness to surf alone. For advanced surfers who value solitude as much as wave quality, Tamri is worth the drive.

What to expect: Multiple reef setups that break on NW and W swells. The waves are generally hollow, powerful, and uncrowded — on a weekday, you might share the lineup with two other surfers or have it entirely to yourself. The trade-off: no facilities, no easy access, no one to help if something goes wrong. Self-sufficiency is mandatory.

Hazards: Isolation is the biggest one. Tamri is remote. If you get injured, help is far away. The reefs themselves hold urchins and sharp rock. Currents can be strong. Always surf Tamri with at least one other person and ideally with a guide who knows which reef is working that day.

Mysteries — The Slab That Chooses Its Days

Mysteries lives up to its name. Located just north of Anchor Point (the exact location is not advertised publicly), this right-hand slab breaks over a rock ledge and only fires on specific swell and tide combinations. When it works, it produces a short, intense, hollow barrel that attracts the best surfers in the area.

Who it is for: Expert surfers only. Mysteries is shallow, critical, and unforgiving. The take-off is late and steep. The barrel section is short but intense. Wipeouts mean contact with rock. There is no forgiving shoulder — you either make the barrel or you go over the falls onto the ledge.

Finding it: Ask your guide. Mysteries is not signposted, not on Google Maps, and not visible from the road. Local knowledge is the only way to find it and to know when it is working. This is where booking a camp with experienced local guides pays off in ways that no amount of internet research can replicate.

Imsouane Cathedral — Africa’s Longest Wave

Imsouane Cathedral (the main point, not the Bay) is not technically an “advanced” wave in the sense of being dangerous — it breaks over a sandy bottom and is generally forgiving. But it belongs on this list because, on its day, it offers the longest ride in Africa (up to 600+ metres) and demands a specific set of skills: sustained paddling, reading a slowly peeling point break, and maintaining speed through multiple flat sections.

Why it challenges advanced surfers: The length of the ride is both the appeal and the challenge. Most surfers are not fit enough to surf a 500-metre wave without their legs burning by the 300-metre mark. Maintaining speed through the slow sections requires high-line positioning and constant pumping. On a good day, the crowd at Cathedral can be intense — 50+ surfers competing for waves.

Best conditions: NW swell 4–6 ft at 12–14 seconds, mid-tide rising. On these days, Cathedral delivers rides that feel endless and leave you paddling back out with a grin that lasts all day.

Advanced Spot Checklist

  • Check surf forecasts (Windy, Surfline, Windguru) before committing to a remote break
  • Surf with at least one other person at Draculas, Boilers, Tamri, and Mysteries
  • Wear reef booties at all reef breaks — non-negotiable
  • Bring a step-up board for overhead+ days
  • Know the exit before you paddle out
  • Respect local surfers — they know these waves better than you ever will
  • Hire a guide for your first session at any new advanced break
  • Carry a basic first-aid kit in your vehicle (antiseptic, bandages, tweezers for urchins)
  • Do not surf Draculas, Mysteries, or Boilers alone — ever

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the hardest wave to surf in Morocco?
Draculas is widely considered the most dangerous wave in the Taghazout area. A shallow, hollow right-hand slab that breaks over a barely submerged rock ledge, it demands precise positioning, late take-offs, and the ability to handle hold-downs. It only breaks on specific swell and tide combinations, and even many advanced locals give it a miss.
Can advanced surfers surf Anchor Point year-round?
Anchor Point works best from October to April, with the biggest, most powerful swells arriving December through February. Summer (May–September) sees smaller, less consistent swells, though 3–4 ft days still happen. For advanced surfers chasing proper overhead Anchor Point, winter is the only window.
Do I need a guide to surf the advanced breaks in Morocco?
While not strictly required, a local guide is highly recommended for first-timers at advanced breaks like Draculas, Boilers, and Tamri. These spots are remote, condition-sensitive, and have hazards (reef, urchins, currents) that are not obvious from the shore. A guide also knows which break will be working on any given day.
What board should I ride at advanced Moroccan waves?
A 6'0"–6'6" shortboard for most days at Anchor Point and Killer Point. A 6'8"–7'2" step-up for overhead+ swells. At hollow reef slabs like Draculas and Boilers, a slightly wider, thicker board (6'2"–6'6", ~32–34L) helps with paddling into late drops.
