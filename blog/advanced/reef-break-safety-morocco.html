Understanding Reef Breaks vs Beach Breaks

Every surf break falls into one of three categories based on what is underneath: sand (beach break), rock or coral (reef break), or a point of land that the wave wraps around (point break — which can be either sand or reef). Morocco has all three, but its most famous waves — Anchor Point, Killer Point, Boilers, Draculas — are reef breaks or reef-point combinations.

The fundamental difference: a beach break has a sandy bottom that shifts with every swell. The wave changes shape from day to day, sometimes from hour to hour. Wipeouts are generally soft and low-consequence. A reef break has a fixed rock or reef bottom that does not move. The wave is consistent and predictable, but the bottom is hard, sharp, and often shallow. A wipeout on reef can mean cuts, bruises, broken boards, and in the worst cases, head injuries.

Reef breaks produce better waves — that is why advanced surfers seek them out. But the risk-reward equation shifts dramatically as the swell size increases. Understanding the specific hazards of each Moroccan reef break is not optional; it is essential.

Anchor Point Reef Explained

Anchor Point is the best-known reef break in Morocco, and its reef layout is well documented. Understanding it will help you navigate the wave safely and avoid the most common injuries.

The take-off zone sits over a rock and reef shelf that extends from the base of the cliff. At low tide, this shelf can be as little as 30 cm below the surface in places. The shelf is not uniform — it slopes, has ledges, and in some spots drops off sharply. The reef is mostly volcanic rock covered in a thin layer of marine growth that is razor-sharp when scraped against at speed.

The mid-section of Anchor Point transitions to a mix of rock and sand, which is more forgiving. The inside section near the beach is mostly sand. This is why wipes on the take-off at Anchor Point are the most dangerous — you are falling onto the shallowest, sharpest part of the reef with the most wave energy behind you.

Local Knowledge

The reef at Anchor Point has one particularly nasty section about 20 metres from the take-off, where the rock forms a shallow ledge. Locals call it “the surgeon’s table” — a wipeout here almost guarantees a reef cut. The ledge is most exposed at low tide. Paddle wide of this area when you are navigating the lineup.

How to Fall Safely on Reef

How you fall on reef is the difference between paddling back out and heading to a clinic. Here are the rules:

  • Go flat, not vertical. Never dive head-first or jump feet-first off your board. A head-first entry onto reef is how traumatic brain injuries happen. A feet-first entry can result in a broken ankle or a deep urchin wound. The safest fall is a flat starfish: spread your arms and legs wide to distribute your body weight over the largest possible area. This minimises the chance of hitting a single sharp point.
  • Cover your head. As you fall, wrap your arms around your head. Your skull is the most important thing to protect. Everything else heals.
  • Do not fight the wave. When you wipe out on reef, the wave is stronger than you. Let it carry you. Fighting expends energy and increases the chance that you will twist or hyper-extend a joint against a rock. Go limp, protect your head, and wait for the energy to dissipate.
  • Know the depth before you surf. Ask your guide or a local how deep the reef is at the current tide. If the answer is “less than a metre,” treat every wave with extreme caution.
Critical Rule

Never, ever bail your board by jumping off feet-first at a reef break. This is the most common cause of serious foot and ankle injuries in surfing. If you must bail, fall sideways or backwards — never straight down.

Booties Guide — What to Wear

Reef booties are the single most important piece of safety equipment for surfing Moroccan reef breaks. Here is what to look for:

  • Thickness: 3 mm is the sweet spot for Morocco. Thick enough to protect against urchins and sharp rock, thin enough to maintain board feel. Avoid 5 mm booties — they are too bulky and reduce sensitivity.
  • Sole: Look for a reinforced rubber sole with good grip. The rocks at Anchor Point and Killer Point are often slippery with algae, especially at low tide. A grippy sole prevents slips during entry and exit.
  • Fit: Booties should fit snugly with no gaps. Water will fill them regardless, but loose booties create drag and can come off in a heavy wipeout. Try them on with the wetsuit you will be wearing — some surfers need a size up to accommodate the wetsuit leg.
  • When to wear: At all reef breaks, always. At sandy beach breaks (Banana Beach, Crocodile Beach, Panoramas), optional but recommended in winter for warmth.

Sea Urchins — What to Do If You Step on One

Sea urchins are the most common injury at Moroccan reef breaks. They live in shallow rocky areas — exactly where you enter and exit the water at Anchor Point, Killer Point, and Boilers. Stepping on one results in multiple brittle black spines embedded in your foot, causing immediate sharp pain and, over the following hours, a throbbing ache as the venom takes effect.

Immediate Treatment

  1. Get out of the water. Sit down. Do not try to continue surfing — the pain will worsen and you risk pushing the spines deeper.
  2. Do not pull the spines out. Sea urchin spines are extremely brittle. If you grip them with tweezers and pull, they will snap, leaving fragments deep in your foot that are much harder to remove.
  3. Soak in hot water. This is the most effective treatment. Immerse the affected area in water as hot as you can tolerate (not scalding) for 30–60 minutes. The heat denatures the protein-based venom, significantly reducing pain. It also softens the spines, making them easier to remove.
  4. Remove visible spine tips. After soaking, use sterilised tweezers to remove any spine ends that are protruding from the skin. Do not dig for fragments — leave those to a medical professional.
  5. Disinfect and monitor. Clean the wound with antiseptic. Apply a sterile bandage. Watch for signs of infection over the following days: increasing redness, swelling, warmth, or pus. If any of these appear, seek medical attention.
Did You Know?

The old myth about urinating on an urchin wound to dissolve the spines is false. Vinegar can help dissolve smaller fragments, but hot water is far more effective for pain relief. Skip the folk remedies and go straight for the hot water soak.

First Aid for Reef Cuts

Reef cuts are not like ordinary cuts. The rock and marine growth that cause them introduce bacteria, tiny shell fragments, and sometimes coral polyps into the wound. If not cleaned properly, reef cuts become infected quickly and heal slowly.

What to Carry

  • Antiseptic solution (Betadine or similar)
  • Sterile gauze pads and medical tape
  • Tweezers (for urchin spines and debris)
  • Antibiotic ointment
  • Butterfly closure strips (for deeper cuts)
  • Pain relief (ibuprofen or paracetamol)

Treatment Steps

  1. Clean thoroughly. Rinse the wound with clean water or antiseptic solution. Remove any visible debris (sand, rock fragments, marine growth) with sterilised tweezers. Do not be gentle — reef cuts must be cleaned aggressively to prevent infection.
  2. Apply pressure to stop bleeding. Most reef cuts bleed profusely because the wound is often ragged rather than clean. Apply firm pressure with a sterile gauze pad for 5–10 minutes.
  3. Disinfect again. Once bleeding has slowed, apply antiseptic solution or antibiotic ointment.
  4. Cover and monitor. Apply a sterile bandage. Change it daily. If the wound shows signs of infection (redness spreading, warmth, pus, increased pain) within 24–48 hours, see a doctor. Reef infections can become serious quickly.
Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

Keep a small first-aid kit in your day bag. In Taghazout, there is a pharmacy on the main street that stocks antiseptic, bandages, and tweezers. For anything beyond a minor cut, there is a clinic in Aourir (10 minutes from Taghazout) and a hospital in Agadir (45 minutes). Do not ignore a reef cut that is not healing.

Low Tide vs High Tide Hazards

Tide changes the risk profile of every reef break. Understanding these differences will keep you safe.

Low Tide

At low tide, the reef is closest to the surface — sometimes centimetres below it. The wave becomes hollower and faster, but also more dangerous. The take-off is steeper. The reef is more exposed. Entry and exit become trickier because you are walking on slippery, urchin-covered rocks. At Anchor Point, low tide is when “the surgeon’s table” is most dangerous. Avoid low-tide sessions at reef breaks unless you are an experienced reef surfer who knows the specific break well.

High Tide

High tide is generally safer at reef breaks because there is more water between you and the reef. However, it brings its own hazards: the wave can become fat and slow at dead high tide, making late take-offs more likely. Some reef breaks (like Boilers) stop breaking entirely at high tide because the water is too deep. And at very high tides, the entry and exit points you used at mid-tide may be underwater, forcing you to navigate unfamiliar terrain.

The Safe Window

Mid-tide, especially a rising tide, is the safest and best window for reef breaks. There is enough water over the reef to provide a safety margin, but not so much that the wave loses its shape. Most reef-related injuries happen at low tide. Time your sessions accordingly.

Safe Entry & Exit at Reef Breaks

More injuries happen during entry and exit than during actual surfing. Slippery rocks, hidden urchins, and unexpected waves make the walk in and out the most dangerous part of a reef-break session. Here is how to do it safely:

  • Wear booties. This is the single most effective thing you can do. They protect against urchins, sharp rocks, and slips.
  • Watch other surfers. Before you enter, watch how locals get in and out. They know the safe path. Follow their route exactly.
  • Time the sets. Do not enter or exit during a set. Wait for a lull, then move quickly and deliberately. Do not stop halfway — commit and go.
  • Carry your board, do not drag it. Dragging your board over rocks damages the board and makes noise that signals you are a kook. Carry it under your arm or on your head.
  • Know the exit before you paddle out. Before you enter the water, identify your exit point. On a big day, the exit you planned to use might be washed out by the time you come in. Have a backup exit in mind.

Reef Break Safety Checklist

  • Wear 3mm reef booties at all reef breaks — non-negotiable
  • Know the tide — avoid low-tide sessions if you are new to the break
  • Watch locals enter and exit — follow their path
  • Fall flat (starfish), cover your head, never dive or jump feet-first
  • Carry a first-aid kit: antiseptic, gauze, tweezers, antibiotic ointment
  • If you step on an urchin: hot water soak, do not pull spines
  • Clean reef cuts aggressively — infection risk is high
  • Surf with a buddy — never solo at a reef break
  • Ask your guide about the reef layout before your first paddle-out

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a reef break and how is it different from a beach break?
A reef break is a wave that breaks over a rock, coral, or reef bottom. Reef breaks produce more predictable, higher-quality waves than beach breaks because the bottom contour is fixed. However, they are much more dangerous — wiping out on reef can cause serious cuts, and the shallow water increases the risk of hitting the bottom.
Do I need booties for surfing in Morocco?
Yes, at any reef break. Anchor Point, Killer Point, Draculas, Boilers, and Tamri all break over rock or reef. Booties protect against sharp rocks, sea urchins, and reef cuts. They also provide grip on slippery rocks during entry and exit. At sandy beach breaks (Banana Beach, Crocodile Beach), booties are optional.
What should I do if I step on a sea urchin?
Do not try to pull the spines out — they are brittle and will break, leaving fragments embedded. Soak the affected area in hot water (as hot as you can tolerate) for 30–60 minutes. The heat denatures the venom and softens the spines. Remove any visible spine tips with sterilised tweezers. If spines are deep, if there are signs of infection, or if you feel numbness or difficulty breathing, seek medical attention.
How do I fall safely on a reef break?
Never dive head-first. Never jump feet-first off your board — you can land on uneven reef and twist or break an ankle. The safest way to fall is to go flat: spread your body out like a starfish to distribute your weight and avoid hitting any single point. Cover your head with your arms. Let the wave carry you rather than fighting it.
