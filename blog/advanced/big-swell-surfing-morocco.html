What Changes at 8 ft+ vs 3–5 ft

Surfing at 3–5 ft and surfing at 8 ft+ are almost different sports. The jump is not just about wave height — it is about everything that height brings with it. Understanding these differences is the first step toward surfing big winter swells safely.

Wave Energy

Wave energy scales exponentially with height. An 8 ft wave carries roughly four times the energy of a 4 ft wave. This means everything hits harder: the white water when you are paddling out, the lip when you wipe out, and the hold-down when you go over the falls. You cannot muscle through an 8 ft wall of white water the way you can at 4 ft. Technique — specifically, a deep, well-timed duck dive — becomes non-negotiable.

Paddle-Out Difficulty

At 3–5 ft, paddling out at most Moroccan point breaks is a 5–10 minute effort. At 8 ft+, the same paddle-out can take 20–30 minutes, especially if you mistime the sets. The current running along the point strengthens significantly. The impact zone extends further out. The lulls between sets feel shorter because each set takes longer to pass.

Consequences

At 3–5 ft, a wipeout is usually a non-event. At 8 ft+, a wipeout on the take-off at Anchor Point or Boilers means you are going over the falls onto reef. A two-wave hold-down is a real possibility. Your leash can snap. Your board can break. You need to be mentally prepared for these outcomes and physically capable of handling them when they happen.

Reality Check

If you have never surfed waves over 6 ft, do not make Morocco your first big-wave experience without a guide and without spending several days building up through the sizes. The North Atlantic in winter is no place to discover your limits.

Equipment for Big Swells

Your everyday 6'0" shortboard and 6ft leash will not cut it when the swell jumps to 8 ft+. Here is what changes:

The Step-Up Board

A step-up is a slightly longer, slightly thicker version of your daily shortboard. For Moroccan winter swells, you want a 6'8"–7'2" step-up with roughly 2–4 litres more volume than your standard shortboard. The extra length helps with paddling speed and early wave entry — on a big, fast-moving swell, you need to catch the wave earlier than you are used to. The extra volume helps you paddle through the impact zone and into position.

Leash Upgrade

Your standard 6 ft leash is designed for everyday conditions. On a big day, upgrade to a 7 ft competition leash or a dedicated big-wave leash. The extra length gives your board more room to travel when you wipe out, reducing the risk of the leash snapping and leaving you swimming. Check your leash for wear before every big-day session — a snapped leash on an 8 ft day at Anchor Point is a long, exhausting, and potentially dangerous swim.

Wetsuit

Moroccan winter water temperatures hover around 16–17°C. A 4/3 mm fullsuit is essential. Some surfers add a 1 mm neoprene vest underneath on the coldest January days. Hoods are rarely needed but can provide comfort during dawn patrol. Booties (3 mm reef booties) are mandatory at all reef breaks regardless of temperature.

Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

Bring two leashes. On a two-week winter trip, the odds of snapping one are higher than you think. A spare leash costs €30 and weighs nothing in your luggage. Not having one can cost you an entire day of an overhead swell.

Paddling Fitness — Are You Ready?

Paddling fitness is the single biggest factor that separates surfers who score on big days from those who watch from the cliff. On an 8 ft+ day at Anchor Point, you will paddle almost continuously for two hours: against the current to hold position, into position for waves, under sets, and back out after rides. If your shoulders burn after 45 minutes of normal surfing, you are not fit enough for a big-day session.

Fitness Baseline

  • Swim test: You should be able to swim 400 metres in open ocean without stopping. This is not a pool swim — the ocean adds current, chop, and cold water to the equation.
  • Breath-hold: You should be able to hold your breath for at least 30 seconds while physically exerting yourself. Practice this in a pool: swim 25 metres underwater, surface, repeat. On a big day, a two-wave hold-down can last 15–20 seconds. Panic is what makes it dangerous, not the duration itself.
  • Session endurance: You should be able to surf (paddle, catch waves, paddle back out) for two hours without your paddling form breaking down. When your form goes, your duck dives get shallow, and you start getting caught inside.

Pre-Trip Training (4 Weeks Before)

If you have a Moroccan winter trip booked, start training four weeks before. Swim 3 times per week, focusing on distance (build to 1 km per session). Add paddling-specific exercises: lat pull-downs, bent-over rows, and shoulder press. Practice breath-hold drills in a pool. The fitter you arrive, the more waves you will catch and the safer you will be.

Honest Advice

If you have not surfed in two months and you book a January trip to Morocco hoping to charge Anchor Point at 8 ft, you are setting yourself up for a frustrating — and potentially dangerous — experience. Respect the ocean. Train before you travel.

Safety: Hold-Downs, Rips & Reef Awareness

Hold-Downs

A hold-down is when a wave holds you underwater for longer than you expect. On a big day at a reef break, a two-wave hold-down can be 15–20 seconds. The key to surviving hold-downs is mental, not physical: stay calm, conserve oxygen, and do not fight the turbulence. Let the wave rag-doll you — resisting only wastes oxygen. When the turbulence stops, orient yourself (follow your leash or look for light), and surface in a controlled way. Take one breath, look for the next wave, and dive again if necessary.

Rips

Rip currents intensify dramatically on big swells. At Anchor Point, the rip that runs along the point can sweep you hundreds of metres in minutes. Do not fight a rip head-on — you will lose. Paddle parallel to the shore (across the rip, not against it) until you are out of the current, then head back to the peak. Alternatively, use the rip to your advantage: at many point breaks, the rip can carry you back out to the take-off zone if you know where it runs. Ask your guide.

Reef Awareness

On big days, the reef behaves differently. Waves break further out, exposing inner reef sections that are normally safely submerged. A spot you know well at 4 ft can have rocks in unexpected places at 8 ft. Know the reef layout at low tide before you surf it at high tide. Wear booties. If you fall, cover your head with your arms and try to land flat rather than feet-first (which can result in a twisted ankle on uneven reef).

Pre-Session Preparation

Night Before

Check the forecast (swell size, period, direction, wind, tide). Charge your phone. Pack your bag: step-up board, 7ft leash, booties, 4/3mm wetsuit, zinc, water bottle, snack. Get a full night’s sleep.

06:00

Wake up. Eat a light breakfast (banana, porridge, toast — nothing heavy). Hydrate. Your guide checks conditions and makes the spot call.

06:45

Arrive at the spot. Watch the lineup for 10 minutes. Identify the peak, the channel, the rip, and any hazards. Stretch on the beach for 5 minutes.

07:00

Paddle out. Time the sets. Use the channel. Stay calm and pace yourself — do not sprint the paddle-out.

09:30

Come in. Two to two-and-a-half hours of big-wave surfing is plenty. Do not surf to exhaustion — that is when mistakes happen.

Big Swell Checklist

  • Check the forecast the night before and again in the morning
  • Bring a step-up board (6'8"–7'2") and a 7ft big-wave leash
  • Wear a 4/3mm wetsuit and 3mm reef booties
  • Surf with a buddy or guide — never alone on a big day
  • Watch the lineup for 10 minutes before paddling out
  • Know where the channel is and use it to paddle out
  • If caught inside, stay calm, dive deep, and wait for the turbulence to pass
  • Limit sessions to 2–2.5 hours — fatigue causes mistakes
  • Bring a spare leash and a basic first-aid kit

Frequently Asked Questions

How big do the waves get in Morocco in winter?
During peak winter (December–February), the Taghazout coast regularly sees swells of 6–10 ft with periods of 12–18 seconds. On the biggest days, sets can push 12–15 ft at exposed breaks like Anchor Point and Boilers. These are serious, powerful groundswells that demand respect and preparation.
What equipment do I need for big winter swells in Morocco?
A step-up board (6'8"–7'2", slightly more volume than your daily shortboard), a 4/3mm wetsuit, reef booties, a 7ft leash (stronger than your standard 6ft leash), and high-SPF zinc sunscreen. Some advanced surfers also carry a safety whistle and a basic first-aid kit in their vehicle.
How fit do I need to be to surf big Moroccan waves?
Very. A big day at Anchor Point can involve paddling against a strong current for two hours, duck diving under 6–8 ft walls of white water, and handling hold-downs without panicking. You should be able to swim 400 metres in open ocean comfortably and hold your breath for at least 30 seconds under physical stress before attempting big-wave sessions.
What should I do if I get caught inside on a big day?
Stay calm. Conserve your energy. If a set is approaching and you cannot make it over the first wave, take a deep breath, ditch your board if necessary (only if no one is behind you), and dive as deep as possible. Let the turbulence pass, then surface calmly. If there are multiple waves, stay under as long as you safely can between waves. Do not panic — panic consumes oxygen faster than anything else.
