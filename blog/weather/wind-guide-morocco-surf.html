Why Wind Matters for Surfing

In Morocco, wind makes or breaks a surf session. You can have perfect swell, the right tide, and an empty lineup — but if the wind is wrong, the waves will be a choppy, disorganised mess. Conversely, even a modest swell can produce exceptional waves when the wind is cooperating. Local surfers in Taghazout will tell you: the first thing they check in the morning is not the swell report — it is the trees outside their window.

Wind affects surfing in two fundamental ways. First, it determines the surface texture of the wave — a clean, groomed face versus a bumpy, crumbling mess. Second, it affects your ability to paddle and position yourself — strong wind can push you out of the take-off zone or make paddling into waves significantly harder.

Did You Know?

The word "offshore" in surfing refers to wind blowing from the land toward the ocean. It has nothing to do with being far from shore — it simply means the wind is coming off the shore. An offshore wind is a surfer's best friend, creating smooth, glassy waves that hold their shape beautifully.

Offshore vs Onshore — What’s the Difference?

Understanding wind terminology is the first step to reading conditions like a local. Here is a simple breakdown:

Offshore Wind

Offshore wind blows from the land toward the sea. In Morocco, this is typically an east or southeast wind. As it blows offshore, it pushes against the incoming swell, holding the wave face open and creating smooth, glassy surfaces. The wave stands up longer before breaking, the lip throws more cleanly, and barrels become more makeable. Offshore wind is universally considered the best wind direction for quality surfing.

Visual cue: Spray blowing off the back of the wave as it breaks. The wave face looks smooth and polished, like dark glass. You can see the wave's features clearly — sections, ramps, barrels.

Onshore Wind

Onshore wind blows from the sea toward the land. In Morocco, this is typically a northwest or west wind — the common afternoon sea breeze. Onshore wind pushes the top of the wave forward, causing it to crumble and break prematurely. The wave face becomes bumpy (called "chop" or "texture"), and the clean lines you see in offshore conditions disappear.

Visual cue: The ocean surface looks rough and textured. Waves break in sections rather than peeling cleanly. Whitecaps appear even on small days.

Cross-Shore Wind

Cross-shore wind blows parallel to the coast. Its effect depends on the specific break orientation. A cross-shore wind may be offshore at one break and onshore at another just a few kilometres away. Cross-shore conditions are generally acceptable — not as good as offshore, but far better than onshore.

The Ideal Wind for Moroccan Breaks

Morocco's Atlantic coast runs roughly northeast to southwest. The premium surf breaks — Anchor Point, Killer Point, Hash Point, and most of the Taghazout/Tamraght area — face west to west-northwest. This orientation means that an east or southeast wind (E/SE) blows directly offshore across the wave face.

The E/SE wind pattern is one of the reasons Taghazout became a world-class surf destination. During winter, high-pressure systems settle over North Africa and the Sahara, creating a stable easterly airflow. In the early morning, this wind is usually light (0–8 knots) — just enough to groom the waves without being strong enough to hinder paddling.

When surfing in Morocco, you want to see the wind arrow pointing from the east or southeast with a speed of 0–10 knots. This is the dream scenario: glassy walls, perfect barrel sections, and a clean take-off. On days when this wind pattern aligns with a solid NW swell and a rising mid-tide, you will experience some of the best waves of your life.

Pro Tip from Our Guides

If you wake up and the palm trees are gently swaying toward the ocean (blowing from east to west), grab your board immediately. Those conditions — light offshore wind — rarely last past mid-morning in Morocco. The best waves of the day happen before 9 AM.

The Chergui — Morocco’s Desert Wind

The Chergui (sometimes spelled Sharqi, from the Arabic word for "eastern") is one of Morocco's most distinctive weather phenomena. It is a hot, dry wind that originates in the Sahara Desert and blows westward toward the Atlantic coast. For surfers, the Chergui presents both a blessing and a challenge.

The Blessing

Because the Chergui blows from the east — offshore at most Moroccan breaks — it can create absolutely perfect surfing conditions. The wind holds the wave faces open, creating glassy walls and hollow barrels that last for days. Some of the best surf sessions in Moroccan history have occurred during Chergui events, with waves groomed to perfection by the relentless offshore airflow.

The Challenge

The Chergui is a desert wind, and it brings the desert with it. Temperatures can soar to 35–45°C (95–113°F), even in winter. The wind carries fine Saharan dust and sand that can reduce visibility, coat everything in a layer of grit, and make breathing uncomfortable. The heat is physically draining — long surf sessions become exhausting, and you need to drink far more water than usual. The wind can also be very strong (20–30 knots), making paddling into waves difficult despite the clean faces.

When Does the Chergui Occur?

Chergui events are most common in spring (March–May) and autumn (September–October), but they can occur at any time of year. They typically last 2–5 days and are associated with high-pressure systems over North Africa. Local guides watch the pressure charts carefully — when a strong high settles over Algeria and Tunisia, a Chergui event is likely.

Chergui Caution

During a strong Chergui event, double your water intake, wear UV-protective clothing even when not surfing, and limit sessions to 2–3 hours. The combination of heat, dry air, and physical exertion can lead to dehydration faster than you expect. Our guides always carry extra water in the van during Chergui conditions.

Morning vs Afternoon Wind Patterns

The daily rhythm of wind in coastal Morocco follows a predictable pattern that every surfer should understand:

Morning (Dawn to 11 AM)

The morning hours are the golden window. Overnight, the land cools, and the pressure gradient between land and sea weakens. Wind speeds drop to their lowest point of the day — often 0–5 knots, sometimes completely glassy. Any wind that does blow is typically from the east (offshore) as the land-based high pressure exerts its influence. This is why dawn patrol is a religion in Moroccan surf culture. The combination of light offshore wind, fewer crowds, and the first light hitting the Atlantic is what makes Moroccan mornings special.

Afternoon (12 PM to Sunset)

As the day progresses, the land heats up. Hot air rises over the land, creating a low-pressure area that pulls cooler air in from the ocean. This is the sea breeze — a northwest wind that blows onshore at most Moroccan breaks. Wind speeds typically build from 12 PM onward, reaching 10–20 knots by mid-afternoon. Wave quality degrades, and the lineups thin out as surfers head for lunch and a nap.

Evening (Sunset Onward)

As the sun sets, the land cools and the sea breeze dies down. Wind speeds drop, and the direction may swing back toward offshore. The evening glass-off can produce beautiful, uncrowded surf — often better than the afternoon, though rarely as clean as the morning. Sunset sessions are an underrated Moroccan surf experience.

Local Knowledge

Between 11 AM and 1 PM, there is often a brief window when the morning offshore wind has died but the afternoon sea breeze has not yet filled in. This "glass-off" lull can produce unexpectedly good conditions — worth watching for if you missed the dawn session.

Seasonal Wind Patterns in Morocco

  • October–November: Stable easterly mornings, moderate afternoon sea breeze. Some of the most consistent offshore days of the year. Minimal Chergui risk.
  • December–February: Strongest offshore winds in the mornings. E/SE wind 5–15 knots common at dawn. The afternoon NW sea breeze can be strong (15–25 knots). Winter storms can bring 2–3 days of strong NW wind that ruins multiple breaks — but also builds the swell.
  • March–April: Windiest months overall. Chergui events most likely. Strong sea breezes. Can be frustrating for surfers, but the mornings still deliver. Local guides adjust by starting earlier (6 AM sessions common) and choosing sheltered spots.
  • May–June: Wind eases. Lighter, more variable. Morning sessions often glassy. Afternoon sea breeze still present but weaker. Excellent for beginner surfers.
  • July–September: Lightest winds of the year. Often completely glassy at dawn. The afternoon sea breeze is mild. The most comfortable time for long sessions. Ideal for learning and longboarding.

Best Apps for Wind Forecasting

Here are the apps that Moroccan surf guides use daily:

  • Windy (windy.com): The gold standard. Animated wind maps let you see exactly how wind is flowing along the entire coast. Colour-coded wind speed layers make it easy to spot the morning lulls and afternoon builds. Free version is excellent; premium adds higher-resolution forecasts.
  • Windguru: The longtime favourite of European surfers. Provides detailed hourly wind speed and direction data in an easy-to-read table format. Particularly good for the Moroccan coast — many local surf camps post links to Windguru forecasts on their websites.
  • Windfinder: Reliable alternative with good Superforecast (high-resolution) data for the Taghazout area. Includes wind, swell, and tide in a single view.
  • Surfline: Best for combining wind, swell, and tide into a single surf-quality rating. The camera integration lets you visually verify conditions at major breaks. Paid subscription required for full features.

Our recommendation: use Windy for visual forecasting and Windguru for detailed hourly data. Check both the night before and again in the morning — wind predictions for the Moroccan coast can shift by 20–30 degrees overnight.

Wind Speed — What’s Too Much?

  • 0–5 knots: Glassy. Perfect. Paddle out immediately.
  • 5–10 knots offshore: Light texture on the face, but clean and very surfable. Ideal for most breaks.
  • 10–15 knots offshore: Holding the face nicely but starting to affect paddling. Spray blowing off the lip. Still very good — this is a classic winter morning in Taghazout.
  • 15–20 knots offshore: Strong offshore. Paddling into waves becomes challenging. Spray in your face on drops. Waves are clean but hard work. Better for shortboards than longboards.
  • 20+ knots offshore: Very difficult paddling. Best to find a sheltered spot or wait for the wind to ease. Chergui conditions.
  • 0–10 knots onshore: Acceptable but not ideal. Wave faces will have some texture. Beginners may not notice, but experienced surfers will.
  • 10+ knots onshore: Choppy and messy. Most breaks will be poor. Head to the most sheltered bay you can find, or wait for conditions to change.

A Day in the Wind — Dawn to Dusk

06:00

Pre-dawn. Land is cool, wind is calm. Your guide checks the morning forecast — often the most accurate reading of the day.

06:30–07:00

Dawn patrol. Light offshore wind (0–5 knots E/SE). Glassy waves, clean faces, empty lineups. This is the magic window.

08:00–10:00

Prime session. Offshore wind steady at 5–10 knots. Waves groomed to perfection. The lineup fills with surfers who know the value of an early start.

11:00–12:00

Wind transition. The offshore wind eases. A brief lull before the sea breeze develops. Some of the best wave quality of the day can happen in this window.

13:00–16:00

Sea breeze dominates. NW onshore wind at 10–20 knots. Waves become choppy. Time for lunch, a nap, or a trip to Paradise Valley.

17:00–Sunset

Evening glass-off. Wind eases as the land cools. Often a second window of clean(er) waves. Sunset sessions can be magical.

Wind-Checking Checklist

  • Check wind direction — E/SE is offshore for most Taghazout breaks
  • Check wind speed — 0–10 knots is ideal, 10–15 knots is fine for shortboards
  • Look at the trees outside your window — swaying toward the ocean = offshore
  • Plan around the morning window — dawn to 10 AM for the best conditions
  • Check at least two wind forecast apps (Windy + Windguru recommended)
  • If it is onshore, ask your guide about sheltered alternatives (bays, coves)
  • During Chergui — double your water, wear sun protection, limit session length
  • Recheck the wind mid-morning — the lull before the sea breeze can be excellent
  • Book a surf camp with guides who know the local wind patterns intimately

Frequently Asked Questions

What wind direction is best for surfing in Morocco?
East to southeast (E/SE) wind is the ideal offshore wind for most Moroccan breaks, particularly the Taghazout points. It blows from the land toward the ocean, holding the wave face open and creating clean, groomed conditions. This wind pattern is most common in the early morning.
What is the Chergui wind?
The Chergui (or Sharqi) is a hot, dry wind that blows from the Sahara Desert toward the Atlantic coast. It is an easterly wind and, because it blows offshore at most breaks, it can create perfectly groomed waves. However, it can also bring sand, dust, and extremely hot temperatures (35–45°C), making it physically challenging.
Why is wind so important for surf conditions?
Wind determines the surface texture of the wave. Offshore wind (blowing from land to sea) smooths the wave face and holds it open, creating clean, barrelling waves. Onshore wind (blowing from sea to land) ruffles the surface and makes waves crumble, producing messy, choppy conditions.
When is the wind best for surfing in Morocco?
Early morning, from dawn until about 10–11 AM, is the best window. During winter, the offshore easterly wind is most reliable in the early hours. By midday, the diurnal sea breeze usually kicks in from the northwest (onshore), making conditions choppier. Dawn patrol is a Moroccan surf tradition for this exact reason.
What wind speed is too strong for surfing?
Offshore wind over 15–20 knots becomes challenging — it can hold the face open beautifully but makes paddling into waves difficult and can blow spray into your face. Onshore wind over 10–12 knots creates chop that degrades wave quality significantly. Glassy conditions (0–5 knots) are ideal.
Does wind direction matter differently for each Moroccan break?
Yes. The Taghazout points (Anchor Point, Killer Point) face WNW, so E/SE wind is offshore. Imsouane Cathedral, which faces more north, works best with SE/S wind. Tamraght beach breaks can handle a wider range of wind directions as they are more exposed. Always check the specific break's orientation relative to the wind.
Is there a windy season in Morocco?
Wind patterns in Morocco are seasonal. Winter (Oct–Mar) generally has the most stable offshore wind, especially in the mornings. Spring (Apr–May) can be the windiest season overall, with stronger trade winds and occasional Chergui events. Summer (Jun–Sep) has lighter variable winds with a predictable afternoon sea breeze pattern.
What's the best app for checking wind in Morocco?
Windy is the most popular choice — its animated wind map is excellent for visualising wind patterns along the entire coast. Windguru is widely used by Moroccan locals and provides detailed hourly wind data. Surfline and Windfinder are also reliable. We recommend checking at least two sources before making session decisions.
Can you surf in Morocco when the wind is onshore?
Yes, but quality drops significantly. Onshore wind creates bumpy, disorganised waves. However, some breaks are more sheltered — protected bays and coves can remain clean even when the main beaches are blown out. If the wind turns onshore, head to the most sheltered break your guide recommends.
How does the afternoon sea breeze work in Morocco?
During the day, the land heats up faster than the ocean. This creates a pressure difference that draws cooler air from the sea toward the land — the sea breeze. In Morocco, this typically blows from the northwest (onshore at most breaks) and builds from late morning through the afternoon, dying down after sunset.
