Where Moroccan Waves Come From

Every wave that breaks on the Moroccan coast — whether it is a gentle roller at Imsouane Bay or a thundering set at Anchor Point — started its journey thousands of kilometres away in the North Atlantic Ocean. Understanding where these waves come from and how they behave is the single most important skill a surf traveller can develop before booking a trip to Morocco.

The North Atlantic is one of the most active swell-producing basins on Earth. During the Northern Hemisphere winter (October through April), powerful low-pressure systems track eastward across the ocean between Iceland and the Azores. These storms generate enormous amounts of wind energy over vast stretches of open water — energy that transforms into organised swell as it travels south toward Morocco.

Unlike windswell, which is choppy and disorganised, this groundswell travels efficiently across the open ocean, losing very little energy along the way. By the time it reaches the Moroccan shelf, it has been refined into clean, long-period waves that light up every point break and reef along the coast. This is the fundamental reason why Morocco is one of the most consistent surf destinations on the planet.

Did You Know?

A single North Atlantic storm can generate swell that reaches Morocco, Portugal, France, the Canary Islands, and even the Caribbean — all from one weather system. The Moroccan coast receives some of the purest versions of this swell because it faces the open ocean directly with no landmass in between.

How North Atlantic Storms Generate Swell

To understand Moroccan surf, you need to understand the engine that creates it. The process starts with a low-pressure system — essentially a large area of rising, unstable air — forming over the North Atlantic. As this system intensifies, it generates strong winds that blow across the ocean surface. Three factors determine how much swell a storm will produce:

1. Wind Speed

The stronger the wind, the more energy it transfers to the water. A typical winter North Atlantic storm can produce sustained winds of 40–60 knots (75–110 km/h) over a huge area. These winds physically push against the ocean surface, creating ripples that grow into waves. The longer and harder the wind blows, the bigger those waves become.

2. Fetch — The Distance Over Water

Fetch is the uninterrupted distance that wind travels across open water. In the North Atlantic, fetch lengths of 3,000 to 5,000 kilometres are common — meaning wind can blow across an entire ocean basin without hitting land. This enormous fetch is what gives North Atlantic swell its power. A storm near Newfoundland can generate waves that arrive in Morocco several days later as perfectly groomed groundswell.

3. Duration

Wind must blow consistently over the same area for an extended period. A storm that lasts 24 to 48 hours will produce significantly more swell energy than a quick-moving front that passes in six hours. The most productive setups occur when a deep low stalls in the mid-Atlantic, spinning for days and generating non-stop swell.

When all three factors — strong wind, long fetch, and extended duration — combine, the result is a powerful groundswell that can light up Morocco for a week or more. The wave energy travels away from the storm in all directions, spreading out in circular patterns called swell trains. As it propagates, the swell organises itself: longer-period waves travel faster and arrive first, followed by shorter-period waves. This is why a new swell often starts with clean, powerful sets before filling in with more consistent, smaller waves.

Why Morocco Gets Such Consistent Waves

Morocco sits in a geographically privileged position for surf. Several factors combine to make this coastline one of the most consistent wave-catching zones on Earth:

1. Direct exposure. The Atlantic coast of Morocco faces west-northwest — almost exactly perpendicular to the direction from which the most powerful North Atlantic swell arrives. There is no significant landmass between Morocco and the storm track, so swell energy reaches the coast largely undiluted.

2. The continental shelf. About 30–50 kilometres offshore, the seafloor rises sharply from deep ocean (3,000+ metres) to the continental shelf (200 metres). This abrupt change in depth causes incoming swell to bend, focus, and steepen — amplifying wave height at spots like Anchor Point and Killer Point. This bathymetric focusing is one reason why Taghazout's point breaks punch above their weight compared to what the raw swell height would suggest.

3. Multiple swell windows. Moroccan breaks don't rely on a single swell direction. NW swell (300°–320°) lights up Taghazout and Tamraght. NNW swell (320°–340°) feeds Imsouane and points further north. W swell (270°–290°) works best for beach breaks and some of the southern points near Agadir. This means that even when the swell direction shifts, somewhere along the coast will be working.

4. Year-round activity. While winter (October–April) is the prime swell season, the North Atlantic never goes completely quiet. Summer sees smaller but still rideable swell, especially from Azores highs that brush the coast with 2–4 ft waves. This year-round consistency is rare among surf destinations.

5. Headland refraction. The Moroccan coastline is punctuated by rocky headlands that act as natural wave magnets. As swell approaches, these headlands refract (bend) the wave energy, concentrating it onto the points while sheltering the bays. This creates a variety of wave types within a short distance — from powerful point breaks to mellow beach breaks.

Pro Tip from Our Guides

When you see a large North Atlantic low on the pressure charts — especially one sitting between Iceland and the Azores with isobars packed tightly together — book your flights. That system will deliver world-class waves to Morocco within 3–5 days.

Fetch, Period & Direction Explained Simply

If you have ever looked at a surf forecast and felt confused by the numbers, you are not alone. Here is a simple breakdown of the three most important swell metrics and what they actually mean for Moroccan surf:

Swell Height (Feet)

Swell height refers to the open-ocean wave height — not the wave face height at the break. A 4 ft swell at 14 seconds can produce head-high to overhead waves at Anchor Point, while a 4 ft swell at 7 seconds might barely produce waist-high surf. Always check the swell height in combination with the period.

  • 1–2 ft: Small but rideable on a longboard or foamy at protected spots.
  • 3–4 ft: Fun, accessible waves for most levels at most breaks.
  • 5–7 ft: Solid, powerful surf — head-high to double overhead at the points.
  • 8–10 ft: Big, powerful conditions — advanced surfers only at point breaks.
  • 10+ ft: Extreme swell — most breaks close out, only a few protected spots work.

Swell Period (Seconds)

Swell period is the time between successive wave crests. It is arguably more important than wave height, because period determines how much energy the wave carries and how it will behave at the coast. Longer-period waves travel faster, refract more around headlands, and break with much more power.

  • Under 8 seconds: Windswell — choppy, weak, disorganised. Generally poor for surfing.
  • 8–10 seconds: Short-period groundswell — rideable, can be fun at beach breaks.
  • 10–12 seconds: Moderate groundswell — good for most breaks, especially beach breaks.
  • 12–14 seconds: Solid groundswell — the sweet spot for Moroccan point breaks. Powerful, well-organised, wraps into the coast cleanly.
  • 14–18 seconds: Long-period groundswell — very powerful. On a 4–6 ft swell at 16 seconds, expect double-overhead sets at the points. Handle with caution.
  • 18+ seconds: Deep ocean swell — massive energy. Can be overwhelming at exposed breaks but magical at spots that handle size.

Swell Direction (Degrees)

Swell direction tells you where the swell is coming from, measured in degrees clockwise from true north. A 315-degree swell is coming from the northwest. Moroccan coast geography means that different spots light up on different swell directions:

  • 270°–290° (W): Hits the coast head-on. Good for beach breaks, but point breaks may be shadowed or close out.
  • 290°–310° (WNW): Excellent wraparound for most Taghazout points. Clean, long walls.
  • 300°–320° (NW): The goldilocks direction for Taghazout — Anchor Point and Killer Point fire on this.
  • 320°–340° (NNW): Better for northern breaks like Imsouane and points towards Essaouira.
  • 340°+ (N): Mostly blocked by the Iberian Peninsula. Sheltered conditions, small surf.
Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

When checking a forecast, always look at swell period first, then direction, then height. A 4 ft swell at 16 seconds will produce much bigger and better waves than a 6 ft swell at 8 seconds. What Makes a “Good” Swell for Each Spot

Not every swell is created equal, and what works for Anchor Point might not work for Imsouane Bay. Understanding the relationship between swell direction and local geography is key to scoring the best waves on any given day. Here is what to look for at each major break:

Taghazout (Anchor Point, Killer Point, Hash Point)

The Taghazout points are west-northwest-facing and love a NW swell (300°–320°). This direction wraps perfectly around the headland, creating long, peeling walls that hold their shape for hundreds of metres. Too west (below 290°), and the swell hits the coast directly, often closing out Anchor Point. Too north (above 330°), and the headland blocks the swell entirely, leaving the points flat.

Tamraght (Devil's Rock, Crocodile, Spiders)

Tamraght's breaks sit slightly further north and have a wider swell window. They handle WNW to NNW swell (290°–340°) well, with the beach breaks at Devil's Rock and Crocodile offering fun, peaky waves on almost any west-facing swell. On bigger days, these beach breaks can become challenging paddle-outs, while the points to the south clean up.

Imsouane (Cathedral, The Bay)

Imsouane Cathedral (the long point break) needs a NNW swell (320°–340°) to wrap into the bay. The longer the period, the better the wraparound effect — a 14-second period is ideal. The Bay (inside section) is much more forgiving: it handles a wider range of swell directions and is the most reliable wave in Morocco for beginners and longboarders.

Boilers & Killer Point (Big Swell Refuges)

When swell gets truly large (8–10 ft+), certain spots that are normally mediocre come alive. Killer Point can handle more size than Anchor Point. Boilers, a reef break south of Taghazout, needs a big NW swell to break properly but offers a long, challenging right when it is on.

How to Read a Surf Forecast for Morocco

Reading a surf forecast is a skill that improves with practice. Here is a step-by-step approach for checking Moroccan conditions:

Step 1: Check the swell chart. Look at the primary swell height (in feet) and period (in seconds). For Moroccan point breaks, you want at least 10 seconds of period. Anything above 12 seconds is promising.

Step 2: Check the swell direction arrow. Is it pointing from the NW (300°–320°)? That is the sweet spot for Taghazout. NNW (320°–340°)? Head to Imsouane. W (270°–290°)? Stick to beach breaks.

Step 3: Check the wind forecast. Morning offshore (E/SE wind) is ideal. Afternoon onshore (NW/W wind) is less desirable. Many forecast apps show wind direction and speed by hour — plan your session around the offshore window.

Step 4: Check the tide. Most Moroccan breaks work best on a mid-tide. Know whether the tide is pushing in (rising) or dropping (falling) during your planned surf window.

Step 5: Cross-reference multiple sources. Check at least two forecasts (Windy, Windguru, Surfline, or Magicseaweed). If they agree, you can be confident. If they don't, favour the one with the longer period prediction — groundswell is usually under-forecast rather than over-forecast.

Recommended apps: Windy (best for wind visualisation), Windguru (popular with local surfers), Surfline (good surf-specific forecasts with camera integration), and Magicseaweed (reliable swell charts). None are perfect, but used together they provide a reliable picture of what is coming.

Groundswell vs Windswell — What’s the Difference?

The difference between groundswell and windswell is one of the most important concepts in surf forecasting, and it is especially relevant to Moroccan conditions.

Groundswell is generated by storms far out at sea — hundreds or thousands of kilometres from the coast. As it travels, the wave energy organises into clean, evenly spaced lines (swell trains) with consistent periods (10+ seconds). By the time groundswell reaches Morocco, it produces smooth, powerful, well-shaped waves that peel beautifully along the points. This is the type of swell you want for Anchor Point, Killer Point, and Cathedral.

Windswell is generated locally by winds blowing near the coast. It has a short period (usually under 8 seconds), is choppy and disorganised, and produces messy, unpredictable waves. In Morocco, windswell is most common in summer afternoons when the sea breeze kicks in from the northwest. While not ideal for the point breaks, windswell can still provide fun options at exposed beach breaks, especially for beginners who don't mind the bump.

How to tell the difference in a forecast: If the swell period is 12+ seconds and the swell height arrives in pulses throughout the day, it is groundswell. If the period is under 8 seconds and the swell height is steady throughout the day (often building with the afternoon wind), it is windswell.

Common Mistake

Many visiting surfers look only at swell height and ignore period. A 6 ft swell at 7 seconds is almost always worse for surfing than a 3 ft swell at 14 seconds. Always check the period first — it tells you more about wave quality than height ever will.

How Tide Interacts with Swell

Tide is the final piece of the puzzle. Moroccan tides are semi-diurnal (two high tides and two low tides per day), with a tidal range of about 2–3 metres. This range is significant enough to make or break a session.

Low tide: Exposed reef and rock at point breaks. Anchor Point becomes dangerously shallow at the take-off. Wave faces can become steep and hollow — exciting for advanced surfers, dangerous for intermediates. Beach breaks become fast and shore-dumpy. Not recommended for beginners.

Mid-tide (rising): The goldilocks zone. Enough water to cover the reef, but not so much that the wave loses its shape. The rising tide pushes water over the reef, smoothing out the wave face and creating long, workable walls. This is when most local guides will tell you to paddle out.

Mid-tide (falling): Similar quality to rising, but the window is shorter — as the tide drops, conditions deteriorate faster. The falling tide can also pull water off the reef, creating strong rips.

High tide: Too much water can swamp out the reef breaks, making them slow and fat. Beach breaks can become "full" — waves lose their steepness and roll through rather than breaking. On a big swell, however, high tide can be necessary to prevent the waves from closing out on shallow reef.

Our recommendation: plan your sessions around a rising mid-tide whenever possible. Check a local tide chart the night before and aim to be in the water an hour or two after low tide.

Seasonal Swell Patterns

Morocco's swell follows a predictable seasonal rhythm driven by the North Atlantic storm cycle:

  • October–November: Season kick-off. First proper North Atlantic lows push swell toward Morocco. Swell 3–6 ft at 10–14 seconds. Water still warm (20–22°C). Growing consistency.
  • December–February: Peak season. Strongest, most frequent storms. Swell 5–10 ft at 12–18 seconds is common. Multiple swell events per month. The best time for advanced surfers chasing size and power.
  • March–April: Transition season. Swell gradually decreases but remains very consistent. Swell 3–6 ft at 10–14 seconds. Water warming (17–19°C). Less crowded. Many locals consider this the best time of year.
  • May–September: Summer season. Storms track north, swell generation decreases. Swell 2–4 ft at 8–12 seconds. Warm water (20–23°C). Perfect for beginners and intermediates. Windswell common in afternoons.

The Journey of a Wave — From Storm to Shore

Day 1

Low-pressure system forms in the North Atlantic between Iceland and the Azores. Winds reach 40–60 knots over thousands of kilometres of open fetch.

Day 2

The storm intensifies. Wind energy transfers to the ocean surface, creating a chaotic sea state. Wave energy begins to organise into swell trains radiating outward in all directions.

Day 3

The fastest waves (longest period, 16–18 seconds) outrun the rest of the swell train. They travel at 40–60 km/h toward Morocco. Forecast models detect the incoming swell.

Day 4

Forerunners arrive. The ocean surface appears calm, then long-period sets begin to show — clean lines marching toward the coast. Early birds score empty lineups.

Day 5

Peak swell. The bulk of the swell train arrives. Waves are at their biggest and most consistent. Anchor Point fires, surfers flock to the points. This is the day you travelled for.

Day 6–7

Swell gradually fades as shorter-period waves arrive. Conditions remain fun but less powerful. The swell train passes, and the coast waits for the next storm.

Swell-Checking Checklist

  • Download at least two surf forecast apps (Windy, Windguru, or Surfline)
  • Check swell height — 3+ ft is generally good for Moroccan breaks
  • Check swell period — 10+ seconds is good, 12+ seconds is excellent
  • Check swell direction — NW 300°–320° for Taghazout, NNW 320°–340° for Imsouane
  • Check wind — offshore E/SE is ideal, especially in the morning
  • Check tide — aim for mid-tide, preferably rising
  • Cross-reference at least two forecast sources
  • Look at pressure charts for North Atlantic lows — deep lows = big swell
  • Ask your surf camp guide — local knowledge beats all forecasts

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do Moroccan waves come from?
Morocco's waves are generated by storms in the North Atlantic Ocean, sometimes thousands of kilometres away. These storms produce swell that travels across the ocean and arrives on Morocco's west-facing coastline.
What is swell period and why does it matter?
Swell period is the time in seconds between successive wave crests. Longer periods (12–16 seconds) mean more powerful, better-organised waves that wrap into points and reefs efficiently. Short periods (under 8 seconds) produce weak, choppy surf.
What swell direction is best for Taghazout?
Northwest (NW) swell at 300–320 degrees is ideal for Taghazout. It wraps perfectly into Anchor Point and Killer Point, producing long, clean right-handers.
Why is Morocco so consistent for surf?
Morocco's west-facing Atlantic coastline is directly exposed to the North Atlantic storm track, which is active from October to April. The long continental shelf and varied bathymetry focus swell energy onto the point breaks and beaches.
What's the difference between groundswell and windswell?
Groundswell is generated by distant storms and travels long distances, producing clean, powerful waves with long periods. Windswell is created by local winds and produces short-period, choppy, less organised waves.
How do I read a surf forecast for Morocco?
Check swell height (ft), period (seconds), and direction (degrees). For Moroccan breaks, look for: swell direction 280–330 degrees, period 10+ seconds, swell height 3–8 ft, and light offshore wind (E/SE). Apps like Windy, Surfline, and Windguru provide this data.
What does 'fetch' mean?
Fetch is the distance over water that wind blows in a single direction. Longer fetch creates bigger, more powerful swell. The North Atlantic has thousands of kilometres of open fetch, which is why Morocco receives such consistent, powerful waves.
Can swell be too big for surfing in Morocco?
Yes. When swell exceeds 10–12 ft, many breaks become unsurfable. Anchor Point closes out, beach breaks become messy, and currents become dangerous. During these periods, protected spots like Killer Point or the inside of Imsouane Bay can still offer manageable waves.
Does swell direction matter for Imsouane?
Yes. Imsouane's Cathedral point break works best on NW to NNW swell (300–340 degrees). The bay (inside section) is more forgiving and handles a wider range of swell directions, making it reliable for beginners and intermediates.
What happens to swell in summer in Morocco?
Summer (May–September) sees smaller, less frequent North Atlantic swell. However, Morocco still receives consistent 2–4 ft waves, perfect for beginners and intermediates. The water is warmer, the winds are lighter, and crowds are minimal.
How accurate are surf forecasts for Morocco?
Swell forecasts for Morocco are generally reliable 3–5 days out, as the swell source (North Atlantic storms) is well monitored. Local wind can change quickly, however — always check the morning of your session for the most accurate conditions.
Do tides affect how swell hits Moroccan breaks?
Absolutely. Many Moroccan point breaks, including Anchor Point and Killer Point, work best on a mid-tide (neither too low nor too high). Reef exposure at low tide can make take-offs dangerously shallow, while high tide can swamp out the wave and reduce its shape.
