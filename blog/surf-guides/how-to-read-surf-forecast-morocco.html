Why Surf Forecasting Matters in Morocco

Morocco sits at the receiving end of the entire North Atlantic Ocean. There is nothing between Newfoundland and Taghazout but 5,000 kilometres of open water, which means the swell that reaches this coast is pure, powerful, and — crucially — predictable. Unlike surfing destinations with complex island chains or fickle local wind patterns, Morocco's surf follows a set of rules that, once understood, make it one of the most forecastable destinations on the planet.

If you learn to read a surf forecast for this coastline, you will know whether Anchor Point will be firing before you even leave your riad. You will know whether to head to Imsouane for mellow longboard waves or to Tamraght for punchy beach breaks. You will know whether to surf at dawn or wait for the afternoon tide push. And most importantly, you will save yourself the frustration of driving 45 minutes to a spot only to find it flat or blown out.

In this guide, our team of Moroccan surf guides shares exactly how we read forecasts every morning — the same process we use to decide which break to take our guests to, and the same logic that helps us score the best waves in the country.

Did You Know?

Morocco's Atlantic coastline receives more consistent swell days per year (250+) than almost any other surf destination in Europe or Africa. The combination of exposed geography and reliable North Atlantic storm generation means you are almost never truly skunked — even in summer, head-high days occur regularly.

Understanding Swell: Height, Period & Direction

Every surf forecast is built on three fundamental variables. Master these three, and you can read any forecast chart in the world.

Swell Height (Wave Height)

Swell height is the vertical distance from the trough to the crest of a wave, measured in open ocean. On most forecast platforms, this is displayed in feet (ft) or metres (m). A 3 ft swell at 14 seconds can produce head-high (4–6 ft) faces at exposed breaks in Morocco. A 6 ft swell at 14 seconds produces overhead to double-overhead waves. The rule of thumb: multiply the swell height by 1.5 to 2 to estimate face height at Moroccan point breaks.

Swell Period

The swell period — the time in seconds between consecutive wave crests passing a fixed point — is arguably more important than swell height. Think of it as the quality and power indicator:

Period RangeTypeQuality in Morocco
Under 8sWindswellChoppy, weak, short rides. Rarely worth paddling out.
8–10sShort-period groundswellFun at beach breaks. Too weak for point breaks.
10–12sModerate groundswellGood at most breaks. Anchor Point starts to come alive.
12–16sLong-period groundswellIdeal for Morocco. Powerful, organised, excellent shape.
16–20s+Very long-period groundswellMassive power. Point breaks fire; beach breaks close out.

At 12–16 seconds, waves in Morocco travel at roughly 30–40 km/h in open water and pile up beautifully on the reefs and sandbars. At periods below 8 seconds, you are looking at local wind chop — fine for messing around on a foamie but not for serious surfing.

Swell Direction

Swell direction is measured in degrees (0°–360°) and indicates where the swell is coming from. In Morocco, the ideal swell direction depends on your chosen break:

  • NW 300°–320°: The sweet spot. Wraps perfectly into Taghazout bay and lights up every point break from Anchor Point to Killer Point.
  • WNW 290°–300°: Still good. Slightly more westerly angle, works well at beach breaks and most points.
  • N 340°–360°: Passes straight by the main points but works well at northern-facing spots and Imsouane.
  • W 270°: Too straight. Tends to close out across the entire bay. Beach breaks become walls of whitewater.
Pro Tip from Our Guides

When a NW swell hits the Moroccan coast, it refracts (bends) around headlands and points before breaking. This means the same swell can produce different wave shapes at Hash Point, Anchor Point, and Killer Point — even though they are within 2 km of each other. Always check the exact swell angle against a spot map; 5°–10° can make the difference between pumping and average.

How to Read a Swell Chart for the Moroccan Coast

Most forecast platforms display swell data in two formats: map view (colour-coded waves animated over the ocean) and spot-specific tables (rows of data for a single location). Knowing how to interpret both is essential.

Map View: The Big Picture

On Windy or Surfline's map view, look at the North Atlantic. You want to see a large area of purple, red, or orange — these colours represent significant wave height. Next, note the arrows. On Windy, swell arrows show direction of travel. You want to see arrows pointing from the northwest towards the Moroccan coast. If the arrows are pointing south, the swell is bypassing Morocco. If they point from the west, expect closing-out conditions.

Spot-Specific View: The Detail

For a specific spot like Anchor Point, look for a table or graph showing: wave height over the next few days, swell period, swell direction, and — crucially — wind speed and direction. Most platforms let you toggle between "swell" and "wind" layers. Always check both. A perfect 6 ft NW swell at 14 seconds is useless if a 25-knot onshore wind is turning it to soup.

Local Forecast Tip

Don't rely on a single forecast model. The Moroccan coast can trick even the best algorithms. Cross-check GFS wind data (on Windy) with WaveWatch III swell data (on Surfline). If both models agree, you can be confident. If they diverge, err on the side of caution and check the beach in person before committing.

Wind: The Make-or-Break Factor

Wind is the single most misunderstood variable in Moroccan surf forecasting. Here is the only rule you need to remember: offshore wind grooms the waves into perfection; onshore wind destroys them.

Offshore vs. Onshore in Morocco

  • Offshore (E/SE): Blowing from the land towards the sea. Holds the wave face open, creates glassy conditions, and allows the swell to stand up before breaking. In Taghazout and Tamraght, offshore wind comes from the east or southeast — the direction of the desert and the Atlas Mountains.
  • Onshore (NW/W): Blowing from the sea onto the land. Makes the wave face crumble, creates chop, and pushes the swell down. This is the prevailing afternoon wind that kicks in around 11 AM–2 PM.
  • Cross-shore (N or S): Blowing along the coast. Adds texture and bump to the face. Generally surfable but messy.

Wind Speed: The Goldilocks Zone

Light winds (0–8 knots) are ideal, regardless of direction — even a light onshore won't ruin a good swell. Moderate winds (8–15 knots) are surfable but you need offshore direction. Strong winds (15+ knots) — if offshore, expect difficult paddling and spray-back; if onshore, do not paddle out unless you enjoy getting slapped in the face by whitewater.

Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

The famous "morning glass-off" in Morocco — that period of dead-calm, perfect waves at sunrise — happens because the land cools overnight, killing the sea breeze. This window typically lasts from dawn until about 10 AM. Our guides wake up at 6 AM specifically to check if the glass-off window is on. When it is, the first two hours of daylight are often the best surf of the entire day.

Tides: When to Paddle Out

Tides along the Moroccan Atlantic coast are semi-diurnal — two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes. The tidal range varies from about 1.5 metres during neap tides to 3.5 metres during spring tides. This matters enormously for wave quality.

Spot TypeBest TideWhy
Point breaks (Anchor Point, Killer Point)Mid-tide, preferably risingLow tide exposes dangerous reef; high tide swamps the take-off. Mid-tide gives the ideal water depth over the reef shelf.
Beach breaks (Panoramas, Banana Beach)Mid to low tideLow tide creates steeper, hollower waves. High tide makes them softer and more beginner-friendly.
Imsouane (The Bay)All tides, but mid preferredThe long sand-bottomed bay works on most tides, but the wave is longest and cleanest at mid-tide.
Imsouane (Cathedral)Mid to high tideNeeds water over the rock shelf. Low tide is dangerously shallow here.

Seasonal Swell Patterns in Morocco

Morocco's surf seasons are driven by the North Atlantic storm cycle. Understanding this annual rhythm is the key to planning your trip:

  • October–November: The season wakes up. The first large groundswells from North Atlantic storms reach Morocco. Water is still warm (19–21°C), wind is often cooperative. An excellent window for intermediates who want size without the full winter fury.
  • December–February: Peak season. The North Atlantic is at its most active. Expect 6–10 ft+ swells at 12–16 seconds, multiple times per month. The wind is predominantly offshore in the mornings. Water drops to 16–17°C. This is when Anchor Point produces its most famous waves. Crowds are at their maximum.
  • March–April: The shoulder. Swells remain consistent but average size drops slightly (4–6 ft). Weather is glorious — sunny, 22–28°C. Water begins warming. Many local guides consider March the single best month: world-class waves, fewer crowds, perfect weather.
  • May–September: Summer season. The North Atlantic calms. Large groundswells become less frequent but still arrive every 7–14 days. Typical summer day: 2–4 ft at 8–12 seconds. Perfect for beginners and intermediates, or experienced surfers who want empty lineups and warm water.

Spot-by-Spot Forecast Guide

Each break on the Moroccan coast responds differently to the same forecast. Here is what to look for:

  • Anchor Point: Needs NW swell (300°–320°) of at least 3 ft at 10+ seconds. Works best on mid-tide rising. Offshore wind (E/SE) is non-negotiable for quality. On a big day (6 ft+), check if Killer Point is the better call — Anchor can max out.
  • Killer Point: Works on bigger swells (5 ft+) when Anchor Point is closing out. Longer period swells (14s+) are best. Needs mid to high tide.
  • Hash Point: Works on smaller, more manageable swells (2–4 ft at 10–12s). A great option when Anchor is too big. Mid-tide, any wind but onshore.
  • Banana Beach (Tamraght): The most consistent beginner-to-intermediate beach break. Works on almost any swell direction from W to NW. Best at 2–5 ft at 8–12s. Mid to low tide creates steeper waves.
  • Imsouane (The Bay): Needs a NW or N swell (320°–350°). Works on a wide range of sizes (2–8 ft). The sand-bottomed bay handles most tides. The longer the period, the longer the ride.

Putting It All Together: A Sample Forecast

Let us walk through a real-world forecast reading. Imagine you wake up in Taghazout on a January morning and check Windy:

  1. Swell: 5 ft at 14 seconds from 310° (NW). Excellent — this is the sweet spot for Anchor Point.
  2. Wind: 4 knots from the east at 7 AM, building to 18 knots from the northwest by 1 PM. Offshore window until late morning, then onshore.
  3. Tide: Low tide at 5:30 AM, high tide at 11:45 AM. Rising tide all morning. Perfect.
  4. Decision: Paddle out at Anchor Point at 7:30 AM. You have a 3–4 hour window of clean, glassy conditions on a rising tide with a perfect NW groundswell. By noon, when the wind swings onshore, move to Panoramas for protected beach break surf. Or have lunch and wait for the evening glass-off.

Common Forecasting Mistakes

  • Mistake #1: Only checking swell size. A 10 ft swell at 6 seconds is worse than a 3 ft swell at 14 seconds. Period is king.
  • Mistake #2: Ignoring tide charts. The difference between a perfect wave and a dangerous reef is often just one metre of tide height. Always check.
  • Mistake #3: Using a single forecast source. Forecasts diverge. Cross-check at least two platforms before committing to a long drive.
  • Mistake #4: Forgetting about local topography. Swell wraps, refracts, and shadows behind headlands. A forecast for "Taghazout" does not apply equally to every break in the bay.
  • Mistake #5: Not looking at the wind forecast beyond the current reading. A glassy 7 AM can turn into a wind-ravaged mess by 10 AM. Check the hourly wind trend, not just the snapshot.
Important Warning

Never rely solely on a forecast when big swell is running. The Moroccan coast has powerful rips, shifting sandbars, and can change character dramatically from one day to the next. If the forecast predicts 8 ft+ at 14 seconds, even experienced surfers should check conditions from the cliff top for at least 15 minutes before paddling out — and ideally consult a local guide.

How to Check the Forecast During Your Day

06:00

Wake up. Open Windy. Check swell height, period, direction for the day ahead.

06:15

Check Windguru for detailed wind forecast — hourly breakdown for the next 12 hours.

06:30

Open tide chart. Note times of low and high tide. Is it rising or falling this morning?

06:45

Cross-check with Surfline spot forecast for your intended break. Do the models agree?

07:00

Step outside. Feel the wind. Look at the sky. Does reality match the forecast? Trust your eyes.

07:30

Arrive at the spot. Watch at least two full sets (10–15 minutes). Confirm wave size and shape.

12:00

Midday check: has the wind swung? Re-evaluate conditions for the afternoon session.

17:00

Evening check: look at tomorrow's forecast. Plan which spot and what time to surf.

Surf Trip Forecast Checklist

  • Download Windy, Surfline, and Windguru apps before your trip (Moroccan SIM data can be slow)
  • Save your local tide chart (bookmark tide-forecast.com for Agadir)
  • Learn to read swell direction in degrees — understand what 300° vs 270° means for your break
  • Always check wind direction AND speed, not just swell. Wind kills waves faster than anything.
  • Cross-check at least 2 forecast sources before driving to a distant break
  • Learn the ideal conditions for your chosen surf spot before arriving in Morocco
  • Watch the ocean from the cliff for at least 10 minutes before paddling out
  • Book a surf guide for your first few sessions — they read the coast better than any app
  • Keep a surf journal: note date, swell, wind, tide, and wave quality. Patterns reveal themselves over time.
  • If the forecast looks marginal, ask your surf camp guide. They know which spots work in which conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best surf forecast app for Morocco?
The best surf forecast apps for Morocco are Windy (best for wind and swell visualisation), Surfline (best for spot-specific forecasts and live cameras), Windguru (preferred by local guides for its accuracy on wind data), and Magicseaweed (now part of Surfline but still useful for swell charts). Most Moroccan surf camp guides use Windy in combination with Windguru for daily spot decisions.
What swell direction is best for Taghazout?
A northwest swell in the 300–320° range is ideal for Taghazout's point breaks, especially Anchor Point and Killer Point. NW swells wrap perfectly into Taghazout bay, creating long, clean walls. Swells from due west (270°) tend to close out across the bay, while swells too far north pass straight by the points.
What is the ideal swell period for surfing in Morocco?
The ideal swell period for Morocco is 12 to 16 seconds. Periods in this range indicate groundswell generated by distant storms, which produces clean, powerful waves with good shape. Anything below 8 seconds is typically local windswell — choppy and less organised. Anything above 18 seconds can be very powerful but may close out on beach breaks.
How reliable are surf forecasts for Morocco?
Surf forecasts for Morocco are reasonably reliable 3 to 5 days ahead, especially for large, well-organised groundswells tracked across the North Atlantic. Wind forecasts become less reliable beyond 3 days. Local guides always cross-check multiple forecast models (GFS for wind, WaveWatch III for swell) and combine them with on-the-ground observation of tides and local conditions.
What does swell period mean in a surf forecast?
Swell period is the time interval (in seconds) between successive wave crests passing a fixed point. A longer period (12–16s) indicates a more powerful, better-organised groundswell. A short period (under 8s) means local windswell — less powerful and often messy. In Morocco, the difference between a 6-second and a 14-second swell can mean the difference between sloppy wind chop and perfect clean lines.
What wind direction is offshore in Taghazout?
In Taghazout, offshore wind comes from the east or southeast (E/SE). This wind blows from the land towards the ocean, grooming the wave faces into clean, glassy walls. The prevailing trade winds bring offshore conditions most mornings, especially in winter. Afternoon sea breezes often swing onshore from the northwest, so dawn patrol is strongly recommended.
How do tides affect surf in Morocco?
Tides have a major impact on Moroccan surf spots. At Taghazout's point breaks (Anchor Point, Killer Point), a mid-tide (especially rising) is ideal — too low exposes dangerous reef, and too high swamps the wave. At beach breaks like Panoramas and Banana Beach, the tide determines whether the wave is steep and hollow (low tide) or soft and mellow (high tide). Always check a local tide chart before heading out.
What season has the best surf in Morocco?
October through April is Morocco's prime surf season, with December to February producing the biggest, most powerful swells from North Atlantic storms. March and April offer slightly smaller but cleaner waves, beautiful weather, and fewer crowds. Summer (May–September) brings smaller but still rideable waves, ideal for beginners and intermediates.
How do I read a swell chart for the first time?
Start by looking at the swell height (in feet or metres), then the period (in seconds), and finally the direction (in degrees or compass bearing). On platforms like Windy, look for colour-coded swell maps — bright reds and oranges indicate bigger swell. Check that the swell direction arrow points towards your chosen break location. Cross-reference with wind arrows — you want wind arrows pointing away from land (offshore). Then check tide tables for the day.
Can I surf in Morocco without checking the forecast?
You can, but you should not. The Moroccan coast is exposed to the full power of the North Atlantic, and conditions change rapidly. A day that looks calm from the beach can have dangerous rip currents, a dropping tide can expose reef at the worst moment, and an unexpected wind shift can turn clean waves into mush. Checking the forecast is a basic safety and surf-quality habit that every surfer in Morocco should adopt.
What is the difference between primary and secondary swell?
Primary swell is the dominant swell train — usually the one with the most energy (longest period and/or largest height). Secondary swell is a second, typically weaker swell arriving from a different direction or source. In Morocco, you often see a primary NW groundswell combined with a smaller secondary N swell — this can create fun peaky conditions at beach breaks but may make point breaks more inconsistent.
How do surf camp guides in Morocco decide which spot to go to each day?
Moroccan surf guides check multiple forecast sources (Windy, Windguru, Surfline) early each morning, then combine that data with real-time observation — stepping outside to feel the wind direction, checking buoy data, and assessing the tide. They match the day's conditions to each spot's ideal requirements (swell direction, tide stage, wind) and the skill level of their group. This is one of the biggest advantages of booking a guided surf trip over trying to navigate the coast on your own.
