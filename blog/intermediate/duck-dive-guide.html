What Is a Duck Dive?

A duck dive is the technique surfers use to pass under an oncoming wave rather than being pushed back by it. Instead of taking the full force of the white water on your chest and board, you sink the board beneath the surface and let the wave roll over you. The result: you emerge on the other side with minimal energy lost and maximum ground gained toward the lineup.

The name comes from the motion — like a duck dipping its head underwater. It is one of the first skills every surfer should learn after being able to catch unbroken waves, because without it, paddling out on anything above waist-high becomes an exhausting, frustrating battle.

Did You Know?

A properly executed duck dive can save you 30–50% of your paddling energy during a two-hour session. On a big Moroccan winter day, that is the difference between surfing for three hours and being exhausted after 45 minutes.

Why Duck Diving Matters in Morocco

During the Moroccan winter season (December–February), the North Atlantic reliably sends groundswells of 6–10 ft to the Taghazout coast. Between sets, you might have 30 seconds of calm. Then a set of five or six waves arrives, and you need to get under every single one of them.

If your duck dive is weak, here is what happens: the first wave catches your board, rips it from your hands, and drags you 20 metres back toward shore. You recover, start paddling again, and the second wave does the same thing. After one set, you have lost 50 metres of hard-earned ground and half your energy. A surfer with a strong duck dive glides under each wave, emerges paddling immediately, and loses maybe 5 metres — sometimes none at all.

At Moroccan point breaks like Anchor Point and Killer Point, the paddle-out can involve navigating a current line and timing the channel between sets. A clean, consistent duck dive is not optional — it is essential for getting out back efficiently and surfing the waves you came for.

Step-by-Step Duck Dive Instruction

The duck dive has four stages. Master each one individually, then combine them into one fluid motion.

Stage 1: The Approach

Paddle toward the oncoming wave with speed. The faster you are moving, the easier it is to punch through the wave. As the wave approaches to within about a metre, take one or two final strong paddle strokes, then place both hands on the rails of the board, roughly at chest level.

Stage 2: Sink the Nose

This is the most critical stage. Push down firmly on the nose of the board with both hands, driving it underwater at roughly a 30–45 degree angle. Your upper body follows the board down. The goal is to get the nose as deep as possible — at least 30–50 centimetres below the surface for a waist-high wave, and deeper for bigger waves. If the nose is not deep enough, the wave catches the board and rips it from your hands.

Stage 3: Drive the Tail

Once the nose is submerged, use your dominant foot or your knee to drive the tail of the board down. This levels the board underwater so it travels horizontally beneath the wave. If you only sink the nose and ignore the tail, the board’s buoyancy will pop the tail up and the wave will catch it.

Stage 4: Glide Under and Resurface

With the board levelled underwater, let the wave pass over you. Keep your body low and close to the board. As the wave passes, the board’s natural buoyancy will bring you back to the surface. Immediately resume paddling — the next wave may be only seconds away.

Pro Tip

Timing is everything. Start your duck dive when the wave is about one metre away. Too early and you resurface into the impact zone. Too late and the wave catches you before you are submerged. Practice until the timing feels instinctive.

Duck Dive vs Turtle Roll — When to Use Each

Not every board can be duck dived. The limiting factor is volume: you need to be physically able to sink the board underwater. Here is a quick guide:

Board TypeDuck Dive?Use InsteadNotes
Shortboard (5'4"–6'6")Yes—Ideal. Sinks easily, fast resurface.
Fish / Hybrid (5'8"–6'8")Yes—Harder than shortboard; use more force on the nose.
Funboard (6'8"–7'6")DependsTurtle roll if too buoyantUnder ~45L, duck dive possible but effortful.
Mini Mal / Midlength (7'0"–8'0")NoTurtle rollToo much volume to sink effectively.
Longboard (8'0"+)NoTurtle rollImpossible to sink. Turtle roll is your only option.
Foamie (any size)NoTurtle rollExtremely buoyant. Do not attempt to duck dive.

The turtle roll (also called the Eskimo roll) involves flipping upside down under your board and holding the rails while the wave passes over the bottom of the board. It is slower and less efficient than a duck dive, but it is the only option for high-volume boards. As an intermediate surfer, transitioning from a foamie or longboard to a board you can duck dive is one of the biggest steps in your progression.

Common Duck Dive Mistakes

  • Not sinking the nose deep enough. This is the number one error. The board stays too shallow, the wave catches it, and you get dragged. Push the nose down until you feel real resistance — deeper is almost always better.
  • Starting too late. If the wave is already on top of you when you begin your duck dive, it is too late. Anticipate and start when the wave is about a metre away.
  • Forgetting the tail. Sinking only the nose leaves the tail sticking up like a sail. The wave catches the tail and flips you. Use your foot or knee to drive the tail down hard.
  • Going too shallow overall. Beginners tend to duck dive almost horizontally just below the surface. You want to go deep — the energy of the wave diminishes rapidly with depth. Aim for at least half a metre below the surface on bigger waves.
  • Not paddling into the dive. Momentum helps. A stationary duck dive is much harder than one with forward speed. Take two strong paddle strokes before each dive.
Common Mistake

Holding your breath too long before the dive. Inhale just before you go under — not 5 seconds earlier. You need maximum oxygen for the few seconds you are submerged, and a late inhale is better than an early one.

How to Practice Duck Diving

Like any skill, the duck dive improves with deliberate practice. Here is a progression:

  1. Flat water (land drill): Lie on your board in waist-deep flat water. Practice the motion slowly — sink the nose, drive the tail, level the board, resurface. Do 20 repetitions. Focus on getting the nose deep and using your foot on the tail.
  2. Small white water: Wade out to where small white-water waves are rolling in (knee-to-waist high). Practice duck diving under them one at a time. The white water provides realistic resistance without the consequences of a bigger wave.
  3. Green waves (small days): On a 2–3 ft day, paddle out through the impact zone and duck dive under unbroken green waves. These are cleaner and easier to dive under than white water.
  4. Bigger days: Gradually work up to bigger surf. Each step up in wave size requires a deeper, more committed duck dive. By the time you are surfing 4–6 ft Moroccan waves, your duck dive should feel automatic.
Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

On a small day at Banana Beach or Crocodile Beach, spend 20 minutes doing nothing but duck dives. Paddle out, duck dive under 10 waves, paddle back in, repeat. It is boring but it works. A surfer who has done 100 deliberate duck dives in one session will never struggle in the impact zone again.

Duck Diving Bigger Moroccan Waves

When the swell hits 6 ft+ on the Taghazout coast, the duck dive requires adjustments. Here is what changes:

  • Go deeper. A 6 ft wave carries energy several feet below the surface. Your duck dive needs to take the board at least a metre deep. Push harder on the nose and hold it down longer.
  • Time the sets. On bigger days, sets come in groups of 5–8 waves with long lulls between them. If you see a set approaching, consider waiting rather than fighting through it. Paddle out during the lull.
  • Use the channel. Most point breaks have a channel — deeper water where waves do not break — that makes paddling out much easier. At Anchor Point, the channel runs along the inside of the point. At Killer Point, it is on the south side. Ask your guide where the channel is and use it.
  • Conserve energy between dives. Between waves in a set, paddle calmly and steadily. Do not sprint between waves — you will exhaust yourself before the set is over. Consistent, steady paddling wins.

Duck Dive Checklist

  • Ride a board you can sink — shortboard, fish, or hybrid under 7'0"
  • Paddle with speed into each dive — momentum helps
  • Sink the nose deep — aim for 30–50 cm below surface minimum
  • Drive the tail down with your foot or knee — level the board
  • Inhale just before you go under, not early
  • Start your dive when the wave is ~1 metre away
  • Practice 20 duck dives in flat water before your first session
  • On big days, use the channel and time the lulls between sets
  • If you ride a longboard or foamie, learn the turtle roll instead

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a duck dive in surfing?
A duck dive is a technique used to push your surfboard under an oncoming wave so you can pass through it with minimal resistance. You sink the nose first, then push the tail down with your foot or knee, allowing the wave to pass over you while you and your board travel beneath it.
Can you duck dive a longboard or foamie?
No. Duck diving works only with boards that have enough volume you can submerge — typically shortboards under 7'0" and funboards under 45 litres. Longboards and foamies are too buoyant. For those boards, use the turtle roll (also called the Eskimo roll) instead.
When should I duck dive vs turtle roll?
Duck dive when riding a shortboard, fish, or hybrid under 7'0". Turtle roll when riding a longboard, foamie, or any board you physically cannot sink. The duck dive is faster and more efficient, so switch to a board you can duck dive as soon as your skills allow.
Why is duck diving important in Morocco?
During Moroccan winter (December–February), swells regularly reach 6–10 ft. Without a solid duck dive, you will spend most of your energy getting caught inside and washed back to shore. An efficient duck dive saves enormous energy and lets you surf longer, better sessions.
How do I practice duck diving?
Start in flat water. Practice sinking the nose with both hands, then driving the tail with your foot. Once comfortable, practice on small white-water waves near the shore. Gradually work your way out to bigger waves. Like any skill, it improves with repetition.
What are the most common duck dive mistakes?
The top mistakes are: not sinking the nose deep enough (wave catches the board and rips it from your hands), pushing the nose down too late (wave hits before you are under), and not using your foot or knee on the tail (board pops back up). Going too shallow is the most frequent error.
