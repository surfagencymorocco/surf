Why Morocco Is Ideal for Progression

The intermediate surfer’s biggest challenge is getting enough quality wave time. Weekend warriors who surf twice a month at their local beach break spend most of their sessions paddling, waiting, and catching closeouts. Morocco flips that equation entirely.

Between October and April, the Moroccan Atlantic coast delivers consistent groundswell that produces rideable waves on roughly 80% of winter days. But consistency is only part of the story. What makes Morocco a progression paradise is the type of waves on offer.

Long point breaks like Killer Point, Imsouane, and the mid-section of Anchor Point provide rides of 100 to 300 metres — sometimes longer. That means an intermediate surfer gets 5 to 10 times more face time per wave compared to a typical beach break closeout. More time on the wave equals more opportunities to practice bottom turns, set up cutbacks, and work on speed generation.

Beyond wave quality, Morocco offers an ecosystem built for progression. Surf camps in Taghazout and Tamraght provide daily transport to the best break for the conditions, meaning you are never stuck at the wrong spot. In-house coaches and video analysis sessions give you immediate, actionable feedback. And the sheer variety of breaks — from mellow sand-bottom points to faster reef sections — means you can progress through difficulty levels without flying to a different country.

Did You Know?

A two-week intermediate surf camp in Morocco typically delivers 20–25 surfed hours. To get the same wave time surfing weekends at home, you would need roughly six months. This compression of practice is why so many surfers return from Morocco noticeably better than when they arrived.

Mastering the Bottom Turn

The bottom turn is the most important manoeuvre in surfing. It is the first turn after the drop and it determines everything that follows: your speed, your line, your positioning on the wave, and your ability to set up the next manoeuvre. A poor bottom turn produces a slow, flat ride. A great bottom turn sets you up for the wave of your life.

What a Good Bottom Turn Looks Like

A proper bottom turn has three phases: the drop, the compression, and the projection. After the drop, you compress by bending your knees and sinking your weight into the board. As you reach the bottom of the wave, you lean into your rail — heelside for a frontside bottom turn, toeside for backside — and drive the board back up toward the lip. The key is committing your entire body to the turn: look where you want to go, rotate your shoulders, and let your lower body follow.

Common Mistakes

  • Looking down at the board. Your board goes where your eyes go. Look down the line, not at your feet.
  • Standing too tall. A straight-legged bottom turn has no compression and no power. Bend your knees until you feel the rail engage.
  • Turning too early. Let the wave set you up. Drop to the bottom of the wave before initiating your turn — turning halfway down the face robs you of speed.
  • Not committing the back arm. Your back arm should open up and guide your rotation. A stiff, tucked arm kills your turn radius.
Morocco-Specific Drill

On the long walls at Killer Point or Imsouane, practice drawing a full bottom turn and then immediately doing a second one on the same wave. Because these waves offer such long rides, you can practice two or three bottom turns per wave instead of one. This repetition is gold for building muscle memory.

The Cutback — Linking Sections

A cutback is how you turn back toward the breaking part of the wave after you have raced out onto the shoulder. It keeps you in the power source and sets you up for the next section. On Moroccan point breaks, where waves can have long, slower sections between the fast parts, the cutback is essential for staying connected to the wave.

How to Execute a Clean Cutback

As you approach the shoulder, shift your weight to your heels (frontside) or toes (backside) and carve a smooth arc back toward the white water. Your upper body leads the turn — open your chest and shoulders toward the direction you want to go. As you reach the foam, use the rebound of the white water to pivot back toward the open face. The entire movement should feel like drawing a figure-eight on the wave face.

Cutback Drill for Moroccan Point Breaks

Choose a wave with a long wall and a clear shoulder. Ride down the line until you are well ahead of the curl, then perform a cutback. After rebounding off the foam, immediately pump down the line and set up a second cutback. Moroccan point breaks like Panoramas and the inside section of Anchor Point are perfect for this drill because they offer multiple cutback opportunities on a single ride.

Common Mistake

The most frequent cutback error is initiating the turn too late. By the time you are on the flat shoulder, there is no wave face left to rebound off. Start your turn before you reach the shoulder — anticipate the section ending and turn early.

Generating Speed on Moroccan Waves

Speed in surfing does not come from the wave alone — it comes from how you interact with it. Generating speed is a skill that separates cruisers from dynamic surfers, and Moroccan point breaks are the ideal classroom.

The High-Line Pump

Rather than riding low on the wave face where the energy is weakest, stay in the top third of the wave. From this high position, use a series of small, rhythmic pumps — shifting your weight from rail to rail while driving your board slightly up and down the face. Each pump converts the wave’s potential energy into forward momentum. At Imsouane, where the wave is famously soft and slow, mastering the high-line pump is the difference between gliding for 600 metres and bogging out after 50.

Using the Bottom Turn to Generate Speed

A well-executed bottom turn is a speed generator. When you drive off the bottom with a fully engaged rail and project upward, you are converting the energy of your drop into forward momentum. The steeper the bottom turn — meaning the more you lean into it — the more speed you generate coming out of it. This is why advanced surfers make bottom turns look explosive while intermediates often look sluggish.

Pro Tip / Astuce SurfAgencyMorocco

Count the pumps. On your next wave at Imsouane or Panoramas, count how many pumps you do between manoeuvres. Aim for three to five quick, tight pumps instead of two big lazy ones. Short, snappy pumps maintain speed better than long, drawn-out ones.

Reading Waves & Positioning

Wave reading is the most underrated skill in surfing. It is also the hardest to teach, because every wave is different. But there are patterns, and learning to recognise them will transform your surfing more than any single manoeuvre.

The 10-Minute Rule

Before you paddle out, sit on the beach or the cliff and watch the lineup for at least 10 minutes. Do not check your phone, do not wax your board — just watch. Pay attention to: where waves are peaking, how many waves come in each set, how long the lull between sets lasts, which ones close out and which ones offer a shoulder, and where other surfers are sitting. This information is free and it will save you an hour of wasted paddling.

Positioning at Moroccan Point Breaks

Point breaks create a predictable take-off zone. The wave always peaks in roughly the same spot — where the swell first hits the point. However, the exact take-off position shifts with the tide and swell size. On a rising tide, the peak tends to move slightly inside (closer to shore). On a dropping tide, it moves slightly outside. Bigger swells break further out; smaller swells break closer in. Watch a full set to identify the current peak before committing to your position.

Local Knowledge

At Killer Point, the peak can shift up to 30 metres depending on the sandbank. After a big swell, always re-assess the lineup — the peak may have moved since your last session. Local guides know the bank positions week to week and will position you correctly straight away.

Video Analysis — The Ultimate Progression Tool

You cannot fix what you cannot see. Most surfers have a mental image of their surfing that bears little resemblance to reality. Video analysis bridges that gap, and it is one of the biggest advantages of booking a coached surf camp in Morocco.

What Video Analysis Reveals

  • Posture: Are you hunched over? Are your arms flailing? Is your head facing the right direction?
  • Timing: Are you popping up too early or too late? Are your turns happening in the right part of the wave?
  • Weight distribution: Are you too far forward (pearling) or too far back (stalling)? Is your weight over the rail when you turn?
  • Missed opportunities: Sections you could have hit but didn’t, cutbacks you could have made, speed you could have generated.

In a typical coached camp, your guide films your morning session from the shore or with a water housing. After lunch, you review the footage together. The guide pauses, rewinds, and draws on the screen to show you exactly what happened and what to adjust. The next session, you paddle out with one or two clear objectives — not a vague intention to “surf better” but a specific goal like “keep my back arm open during the bottom turn.”

DIY Video Analysis

If you are surfing without a coach, ask a friend to film 10 waves on their phone from the shore. Review the footage that evening. Pick one thing to fix tomorrow. Trying to fix five things at once guarantees you fix zero.

Coaching vs Solo Practice

Both coaching and solo practice have their place, and the best progression strategy combines them. Here is an honest breakdown.

The optimal approach for an intermediate surfer on a one-week or two-week camp: morning sessions with a coach for targeted feedback, afternoon free surf to consolidate what you learned. This structure is exactly what most Moroccan surf camps offer, and it is the reason progression happens so quickly here.

Specific Drills for Moroccan Waves

These drills are designed for the types of waves you will surf in Morocco — long point breaks, mellow walls, and beach break peaks. Practice them deliberately, one per session, until they become automatic.

Drill 1: The Double Bottom Turn (Killer Point / Imsouane)

On a long ride, perform a full bottom turn, ride back up to the lip, then immediately drop back down and perform a second bottom turn. The goal is to feel the compression in your legs and the rail engagement on every turn. If you can do three bottom turns on one Imsouane wave, your technique is solid.

Drill 2: The Speed Pump (Panoramas / Banana Beach)

Ride a mid-sized wave and focus exclusively on pumping. Count your pumps out loud. Aim to increase your pump count on each wave. Short, snappy pumps in the upper third of the wave face. Do not worry about turns — this session is about speed generation only.

Drill 3: Cutback-to-Foam Rebound (Anchor Point mid-section)

Take off, race down the line, perform a cutback that goes all the way to the white water, feel the foam push against your board, and use that rebound to pivot back toward the shoulder. The key is committing to the foam — do not bail halfway.

Drill 4: The 10-Wave Observation (any break)

Before paddling out, sit and watch 10 waves break. For each wave, say out loud: where it peaked, whether it opened up or closed out, and where you would have taken off. Then paddle out and test your predictions.

One-Week Progression Plan

Day 1

Assessment session. Coach films you. Focus: identify your top two areas for improvement. Afternoon: video review.

Day 2

Bottom turn focus. Drill: double bottom turn at Imsouane. Afternoon: solo practice consolidating the morning’s work.

Day 3

Cutback day. Drill: cutback-to-foam rebound at Panoramas. Afternoon: free surf with one specific cutback goal.

Day 4

Speed generation. Drill: speed pump at Banana Beach. Focus on high-line positioning and snappy pumps.

Day 5

Wave reading. 10-wave observation drill. Coach positions you in the lineup. Focus on taking the right waves, not just any wave.

Day 6

Integration session. Put it all together at Killer Point — bottom turn, cutback, speed generation on one wave. Coach films for before/after comparison.

Day 7

Celebration session. Free surf at your new favourite break. Video review: compare Day 1 footage with Day 6. See the progress.

Surf Progression Checklist

  • Film your surfing — you cannot fix what you cannot see
  • Focus on one skill per session. Do not try to fix everything at once
  • Watch the lineup for 10 minutes before every session
  • Surf a variety of breaks — points, beach breaks, reefs
  • Book at least 3 coached sessions for targeted feedback
  • Keep a surf journal — write down one thing you learned each day
  • Try different boards — a hybrid one day, a fish the next
  • Stay patient. Progression is not linear — plateaus are normal
  • Book a surf camp with video analysis included

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to go from beginner to intermediate surfer?
Most surfers reach a solid intermediate level after 1–3 years of consistent practice — meaning several sessions per month. In Morocco, where you can surf daily during a camp, progression accelerates significantly. A two-week intermediate camp in Taghazout can produce more improvement than six months of weekend surfing at home.
What is the most important skill for intermediate surfers to learn?
The bottom turn. It sets up every other manoeuvre on the wave. Without a proper bottom turn, you cannot generate speed, set a rail, or set up a cutback. Intermediate surfers who master the bottom turn see immediate improvement across all aspects of their surfing.
Is video analysis worth it for surfing?
Absolutely. Most surfers have no idea what they actually look like on a wave. Video analysis reveals posture issues, timing mistakes, and missed opportunities that are invisible from the surfer’s perspective. One video-reviewed session is worth five unsupervised sessions.
How do I read waves better?
Spend 10 minutes watching the lineup before paddling out. Identify where waves peak, which ones close out, and where the shoulder forms. Track sets — count the waves in each set and the lull between them. With practice, you will start anticipating which wave to take and where to sit.
Why is Morocco good for intermediate surf progression?
Morocco offers long, forgiving point breaks (Killer Point, Imsouane) where intermediate surfers get long rides with plenty of time to set up manoeuvres. The consistency of winter swells means you can surf almost daily, and the variety of breaks — from mellow beach breaks to faster reef points — lets you progress through difficulty levels.
Should I practice alone or get coaching?
A mix of both. Coaching provides targeted feedback and corrects bad habits before they become ingrained. Solo practice builds wave count and confidence. The ideal approach is a coached camp — morning sessions with feedback, afternoon free surf to consolidate what you learned.
How do I generate more speed on a wave?
Speed comes from three things: a well-timed take-off with early entry, a high-line pump in the upper third of the wave face, and using the bottom turn to project up into the power source. Moroccan point breaks are ideal for practicing speed generation because the long walls give you time to build rhythm.
What board should an intermediate surfer ride in Morocco?
A hybrid or fish shape (5'8"–6'4") with 30–38 litres of volume works well for most intermediate surfers in Moroccan conditions. More volume means easier paddling and wave catching; less volume gives you more rail engagement for turning. Most surf camps provide a range of boards to try.
